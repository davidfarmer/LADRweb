    Section 6.1 Inner Products and Norms

    Subsection 6.1.1 Inner Products

    
    Missing description
    🔗
    This vector \(v\) has norm \(\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To motivate the concept of inner product, think of vectors in \(\R{2}\) and \(\R{3}\) as arrows with initial point at the origin. The length of a vector \(v\) in \(\R{2}\) or \(\R{3}\) is called the norm of \(v\) and is denoted by \(\|v\|\text{.}\) Thus for \(v = (a, b) \in \RR{2}\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \|v\| =\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly, if \(v = (a,b,c) \in \RR{3}\text{,}\) then \(\|v\| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Even though we cannot draw pictures in higher dimensions, the generalization to \(\R{n}\) is easy: we define the norm of \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \R{n}\) by
    \begin{equation*} \|x\| = \sqrt{{x_1}^{2} + \dots + {x_n}^{2}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The norm is not linear on \(\RR{n}\text{.}\) To inject linearity into the discussion, we introduce the dot product.
    🔗

    Definition 6.1. dot product.

    For \(x, y \in \RR{n}\text{,}\) the dot product of \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) denoted by \(x \cdot y\text{,}\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} x \cdot y = x_1 y_1 + \dots + x_n y_n, \end{equation*}
    where \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)\) and \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    If we think of a vector as a point instead of as an arrow, then \(\|x\|\) should be interpreted to mean the distance from the origin to the point \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The dot product of two vectors in \(\R{n}\) is a number, not a vector. Notice that \(x \cdot x = \|x\|^2\) for all \(x \in \RR{n}\text{.}\) Furthermore, the dot product on \(\R{n}\) has the following properties.
    1. \(x \cdot x \ge 0\) for all \(x \in \RR{n}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(x \cdot x = 0\) if and only if \(x = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For \(y \in \R{n}\) fixed, the map from \(\R{n}\) to \(\R{}\) that sends \(x \in \R{n}\) to \(x \cdot y\) is linear.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(x \cdot y = y \cdot x\) for all \(x, y \in \RR{n}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    An inner product is a generalization of the dot product. At this point you may be tempted to guess that an inner product is defined by abstracting the properties of the dot product discussed in the last paragraph. For real vector spaces, that guess is correct. However, so that we can make a definition that will be useful for both real and complex vector spaces, we need to examine the complex case before making the definition.
    🔗
    Recall that if \(\lambda = a + bi\text{,}\) where \(a, b \in \R{}\text{,}\) then
    1. the absolute value of \(\lambda\text{,}\) denoted by \(|\lambda|\text{,}\) is defined by \(|\lambda| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. the complex conjugate of \(\lambda\text{,}\) denoted by \(\bar{\lambda}\text{,}\) is defined by \(\bar{\lambda} = a - bi\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(|\lambda|^2 = \lambda \bar{\lambda}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    See Chapter 4 for the definitions and the basic properties of the absolute value and complex conjugate.
    🔗
    For \(z = (z_1, \dots, z_n) \in \CC{n}\text{,}\) we define the norm of \(z\) by
    \begin{equation*} \|z\| = \sqrt{|z_1|^2 + \dots + |z_n|^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The absolute values are needed because we want \(\|z\|\) to be a nonnegative number. Note that
    \begin{equation*} \|z\|^2 = z_1 \overline{z_1} + \dots + z_n \overline{z_n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We want to think of \(\|z\|^2\) as the inner product of \(z\) with itself, as we did in \(\RR{n}\text{.}\) The equation above thus suggests that the inner product of the vector \(w = (w_1, \dots, w_n) \in \C{n}\) with \(z\) should equal
    \begin{equation*} w_1 \overline{z_1}+ \dots + w_n \overline{z_n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If the roles of the \(w\) and \(z\) were interchanged, the expression above would be replaced with its complex conjugate. Thus we should expect that the inner product of \(w\) with \(z\) equals the complex conjugate of the inner product of \(z\) with \(w\text{.}\) With that motivation, we are now ready to define an inner product on \(V{}\text{,}\) which may be a real or a complex vector space.
    🔗
    One comment about the notation used in the next definition:
    1. For \(\lambda\in\C{}\text{,}\) the notation \(\lambda \ge 0\) means \(\lambda\) is real and nonnegative.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Definition 6.2. inner product.

    An inner product on \(V\) is a function that takes each ordered pair \((u,v)\) of elements of \(V\) to a number \(\langle u,v \rangle \in \F{}\) and has the following properties.
    1. positivity.
      \(\langle v, v \rangle \ge 0\) for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. definiteness.
      \(\langle v, v \rangle = 0\) if and only if \(v = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. additivity in first slot.
      \(\langle u + v, w \rangle = \langle u, w \rangle + \langle v, w \rangle\) for all \(u, v, w \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. homogeneity in first slot.
      \(\langle \lambda u, v \rangle = \lambda \langle u, v \rangle\) for all \(\lambda \in \F{}\) and all \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. conjugate symmetry.
      \(\langle u, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v,u \rangle }\) for all \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Most mathematicians define inner products as above, but many physicists use a definition that requires homogeneity in the second slot instead of the first slot.
    🔗
    Every real number equals its complex conjugate. Thus if we are dealing with a real vector space, then in the last condition above we can dispense with the complex conjugate and simply state that \(\langle u,v \rangle = \langle v,u \rangle\) for all \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 6.3. inner products.

    1. The Euclidean inner product on \(\F{n}\) is defined by
      \begin{equation*} \langle (w_1, \dots, w_n), (z_1, \dots, z_n) \rangle = w_1 \overline{z_1}+ \dots + w_n \overline{z_n} \end{equation*}
      for all \((w_1, \dots, w_n), (z_1, \dots, z_n) \in \FF n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(c_1, \dots, c_n\) are positive numbers, then an inner product can be defined on \(\F{n}\) by
      \begin{equation*} \langle (w_1, \dots, w_n), (z_1, \dots, z_n) \rangle = c_1 w_1 \overline{z_1} + \dots + c_n w_n \overline{z_n} \end{equation*}
      for all \((w_1, \dots, w_n), (z_1, \dots, z_n) \in \FF n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. An inner product can be defined on the vector space of continuous real-valued functions on the interval \([-1, 1]\) by
      \begin{equation*} \langle f, g \rangle = \int_{-1}^1fg \end{equation*}
      for all \(f, g\) continuous real-valued functions on \([-1,1]\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. An inner product can be defined on \(\P(\R{})\) by
      \begin{equation*} \langle p, q \rangle = p(0) q(0) + \int_{-1}^1 p' q' \end{equation*}
      for all \(p, q \in \P(\R{})\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. An inner product can be defined on \(\P(\R{})\) by
      \begin{equation*} \langle p, q \rangle = \int_0^\infty p(x) q(x) e^{-x} dx \end{equation*}
      for all \(p, q \in \P(\R{})\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 6.4. inner product space.

    An inner product space is a vector space \(V\) along with an inner product on \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The most important example of an inner product space is \(\F{n}\) with the Euclidean inner product given by (a) in the example above. When \(\F{n}\) is referred to as an inner product space, you should assume that the inner product is the Euclidean inner product unless explicitly told otherwise.
    🔗
    So that we do not have to keep repeating the hypothesis that \(V\) and \(W\) are inner product spaces, we make the following assumption.
    🔗

    Notation 6.5. \(V\text{,}\) \(W\).

    For the rest of this chapter and the next chapter, \(V\) and \(W\) denote inner product spaces over \(\F{}{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Note the slight abuse of language here. An inner product space is a vector space along with an inner product on that vector space. When we say that a vector space \(V\) is an inner product space, we are also thinking that an inner product on \(V\) is lurking nearby or is clear from the context (or is the Euclidean inner product if the vector space is \(\F{n}\)).
    🔗

    Theorem 6.6. basic properties of an inner product.

    1. For each fixed \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) the function that takes \(u \in V\) to \(\langle u,v \rangle\) is a linear map from \(V\) to \(\F{}{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\langle 0, v \rangle = 0\) for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\langle v, 0 \rangle = 0\) for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\langle u, v + w \rangle = \langle u , v \rangle + \langle u, w \rangle\) for all \(u, v, w \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\langle u, \lambda v \rangle = \bar{\lambda} \langle u,v \rangle\) for all \(\lambda \in \F{}\) and all \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. For \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) the linearity of \(u \mapsto \langle u,v \rangle\) follows from the conditions of additivity and homogeneity in the first slot in the definition of an inner product.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Every linear map takes \(0\) to \(0\text{.}\) Thus (b) follows from (a).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then the conjugate symmetry property in the definition of an inner product and (b) show that \(\langle v, 0 \rangle = \overline{ \langle 0, v \rangle } = \overline{0} = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Suppose \(u, v, w \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{align*} \langle u, v + w \rangle \amp = \overline{\langle v + w, u \rangle }\\ \amp = \overline{\langle v , u \rangle + \langle w, u \rangle }\\ \amp = \overline{\langle v , u \rangle } + \overline{\langle w, u \rangle }\\ \amp = \langle u , v \rangle + \langle u, w \rangle \text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Suppose \(\lambda \in \F{}\) and \(u,v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{align*} \langle u, \lambda v \rangle \amp = \overline{\langle \lambda v,u \rangle }\\ \amp = \overline{\lambda\langle v,u \rangle }\\ \amp = \bar{\lambda} \overline{\langle v,u \rangle }\\ \amp = \bar{\lambda} \langle u,v \rangle \text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.2 Norms

    Our motivation for defining inner products came initially from the norms of vectors on \(\R2\) and \(\RR3\text{.}\) Now we see that each inner product determines a norm.
    🔗

    Definition 6.7. norm, \(\|v\|\).

    For \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) the norm of \(v\text{,}\) denoted by \(\|v\|\text{,}\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} \|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v \rangle }. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.8. norms.

    1. If \((z_1, \dots, z_n) \in \F{n}\) (with the Euclidean inner product), then
      \begin{equation*} \|(z_1, \dots, z_n)\| = \sqrt{|z_1|^2 + \dots + |z_n|^2}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For \(f\) in the vector space of continuous real-valued functions on \([-1,1]\) and with inner product given as in Example 6.3(c), we have
      \begin{equation*} \|f\| = \sqrt{\int_{-1}^1 f^2}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 6.9. basic properties of the norm.

    Suppose \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    1. \(\|v\| = 0\) if and only if \(v = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\|\lambda v\| = |\lambda| \|v\|\) for all \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. The desired result holds because \(\langle v,v \rangle = 0\) if and only if \(v = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{align*} \|\lambda v\|^2 \amp = \langle \lambda v, \lambda v \rangle\\ \amp = \lambda \langle v, \lambda v \rangle\\ \amp = \lambda \bar{\lambda}\langle v, v \rangle\\ \amp = |\lambda|^2 \|v\|^2{}\text{.} \end{align*}
      Taking square roots now gives the desired equality.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The proof of (b) in the result above illustrates a general principle: working with norms squared is usually easier than working directly with norms.
    🔗
    Now we come to a crucial definition.
    🔗

    Definition 6.10. orthogonal.

    Two vectors \(u, v \in V\) are called orthogonal if \(\langle u, v \rangle = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The word orthogonal comes from the Greek word orthogonios, which means right-angled.
    🔗
    In the definition above, the order of the two vectors does not matter, because \(\langle u, v \rangle = 0\) if and only if \(\langle v, u \rangle = 0\text{.}\) Instead of saying \(u\) and \(v\) are orthogonal, sometimes we say \(u\) is orthogonal to \(v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Exercise 6.1.2.15 asks you to prove that if \(u, v\) are nonzero vectors in \(\RR{2}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \langle u,v \rangle = \|u\| \|v\| \cos \theta, \end{equation*}
    where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(u\) and \(v\) (thinking of \(u\) and \(v\) as arrows with initial point at the origin). Thus two nonzero vectors in \(\R2\) are orthogonal (with respect to the Euclidean inner product) if and only if the cosine of the angle between them is \(0\text{,}\) which happens if and only if the vectors are perpendicular in the usual sense of plane geometry. Thus you can think of the word orthogonal as a fancy word meaning perpendicular.
    🔗
    We begin our study of orthogonality with an easy result.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.11. orthogonality and \(0\).

    1. \(0\) is orthogonal to every vector in \(V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(0\) is the only vector in \(V\) that is orthogonal to itself.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Recall that Theorem 6.6(b) states that \(\langle 0, v \rangle = 0\) for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(v \in V\) and \(\langle v,v \rangle = 0\text{,}\) then \(v = 0\) (by definition of inner product).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    For the special case \(V = \RR{2}\text{,}\) the next theorem was known over 3,500 years ago in Babylonia and then rediscovered and proved over 2,500 years ago in Greece. Of course, the proof below is not the original proof.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.12. Pythagorean theorem.

    Suppose \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\) If \(u\) and \(v\) are orthogonal, then
    \begin{equation*} \|u + v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(\langle u, v \rangle = 0\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \|u + v\|^2 \amp = \langle u+ v, u + v \rangle\\ \amp = \langle u, u \rangle + \langle u, v \rangle + \langle v, u \rangle + \langle v, v \rangle\\ \amp = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2{}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose \(u, v \in V{}\text{,}\) with \(v \ne 0\text{.}\) We would like to write \(u\) as a scalar multiple of \(v\) plus a vector \(w\) orthogonal to \(v\text{,}\) as suggested in the picture here.

    🔗
    To discover how to write \(u\) as a scalar multiple of \(v\) plus a vector orthogonal to \(v\text{,}\) let \(c \in \F{}\) denote a scalar. Then
    \begin{equation*} u = c v + (u - c v). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus we need to choose \(c\) so that \(v\) is orthogonal to \((u - c v)\text{.}\) Hence we want
    \begin{equation*} 0 = \langle u - c v, v \rangle = \langle u,v \rangle - c \|v\|^2{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The equation above shows that we should choose \(c\) to be \(\langle u,v \rangle /\|v\|^2{}\text{.}\) Making this choice of \(c\text{,}\) we can write
    \begin{equation*} u = \frac{\langle u,v \rangle }{\|v\|^2} v + ( u - \frac{\langle u,v \rangle }{\|v\|^2} v ). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As you should verify, the equation displayed above explicitly writes \(u\) as a scalar multiple of \(v\) plus a vector orthogonal to \(v\text{.}\) Thus we have proved the following key result.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.13. an orthogonal decomposition.

    Suppose \(u, v \in V{}\text{,}\) with \(v \ne 0\text{.}\) Set \(c = \dfrac{\langle u,v \rangle }{\|v\|^2}\) and \(w = u - \dfrac{\langle u,v \rangle }{\|v\|^2} v\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} u = c v + w \andd \langle w, v \rangle = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The orthogonal decomposition 6.13 will be used in the proof of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, which is our next result and is one of the most important inequalities in mathematics.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.14. Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

    Suppose \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} |\langle u, v \rangle | \le \|u\| \|v\|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This inequality is an equality if and only if one of \(u, v\) is a scalar multiple of the other.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    If \(v = 0\text{,}\) then both sides of the desired inequality equal \(0\text{.}\) Thus we can assume that \(v \ne 0\text{.}\) Consider the orthogonal decomposition
    \begin{equation*} u = \frac{\langle u,v \rangle }{\|v\|^2} v + w \end{equation*}
    given by Theorem 6.13, where \(w\) is orthogonal to \(v\text{.}\) By the Pythagorean theorem,
    \begin{align} \|u\|^2 \amp = \lVert \frac{\langle u,v \rangle }{\|v\|^2} v \rVert ^2 + \lVert w \rVert ^2\notag\\ \amp = \frac{\lvert \langle u,v \rangle \rvert ^2}{\|v\|^2} + \|w\|^2\notag\\ \amp \ge \frac{\lvert \langle u,v \rangle \rvert ^2}{\|v\|^2}\text{.}\tag{6.15} \end{align}
    🔗
    Multiplying both sides of this inequality by \(\|v\|^2\) and then taking square roots gives the desired inequality.
    🔗

    Aside
    Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857) proved 6.16 (a) in 1821. In 1859, Cauchy’s student Viktor Bunyakovsky (1804–1889) proved integral inequalities like the one in Example 6.16 (b) . A few decades later, similar discoveries by Hermann Schwarz (1843–1921) attracted more attention and led to the name of this inequality.
    🔗
    🔗
    The proof in the paragraph above shows that the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is an equality if and only if (6.15) is an equality. This happens if and only if \(w = 0\text{.}\) But \(w=0\) if and only if \(u\) is a multiple of \(v\) (see 6.13). Thus the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is an equality if and only if \(u\) is a scalar multiple of \(v\) or \(v\) is a scalar multiple of \(u\) (or both; the phrasing has been chosen to cover cases in which either \(u\) or \(v\) equals \(0\)).

    The next result, called the triangle inequality, has the geometric interpretation that the length of each side of a triangle is less than the sum of the lengths of the other two sides.
    🔗
    Note that the triangle inequality implies that the shortest polygonal path between two points is a single line segment (a polygonal path consists of line segments).
    🔗

    Theorem 6.17. triangle inequality.

    Suppose \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \|u + v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This inequality is an equality if and only if one of \(u, v\) is a nonnegative real multiple of the other.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We have
    \begin{align} \|u + v\|^2 \amp = \langle u + v, u + v \rangle\notag\\ \amp =\langle u, u \rangle + \langle v, v \rangle + \langle u, v \rangle + \langle v, u \rangle\notag\\ \amp =\langle u, u \rangle + \langle v, v \rangle + \langle u, v \rangle + \overline{\langle u, v \rangle }\notag\\ \amp = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2 + 2\re \langle u, v \rangle\notag\\ \amp \le \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2 + 2 \lvert \langle u, v \rangle \rvert\tag{6.18}\\ \amp \le \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2 + 2 \|u\| \|v\|\tag{6.19}\\ \amp = ( \|u\| + \|v\| )^2{}\text{,}\notag \end{align}
    🔗
    where (6.19) follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality Theorem 6.14. Taking square roots of both sides of the inequality above gives the desired inequality.
    🔗
    The proof above shows that the triangle inequality is an equality if and only if we have equality in (6.18) and (6.19). Thus we have equality in the triangle inequality if and only if
    \begin{equation} \langle u, v \rangle = \|u\| \|v\|.\tag{6.20} \end{equation}
    🔗
    If one of \(u, v\) is a nonnegative real multiple of the other, then (6.20) holds. Conversely, suppose (6.20) holds. Then the condition for equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality Theorem 6.14 implies that one of \(u, v\) is a scalar multiple of the other. This scalar must be a nonnegative real number, by (6.20), completing the proof.
    🔗
    🔗

    The next result is called the parallelogram equality because of its geometric interpretation: in every parallelogram, the sum of the squares of the lengths of the diagonals equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the four sides. Note that the proof here is more straightforward than the usual proof in Euclidean geometry.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.21. parallelogram equality.

    Suppose \(u, v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \|u + v\|^2 + \|u - v\|^2 = 2(\|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We have
    \begin{align*} \|u + v\|^2 + \|u - v\|^2 \amp = \langle u + v, u + v \rangle + \langle u - v, u - v \rangle\\ \amp = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2 + \langle u, v \rangle + \langle v,u \rangle\\ \amp + \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2 - \langle u, v \rangle -\langle v,u \rangle\\ \amp = 2 ( \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2 )\text{,} \end{align*}
    as desired.
