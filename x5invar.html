    Section 5.1 Invariant Subspaces

    Subsection 5.1.1 Eigenvalues

    Definition 5.1. operator.

    A linear map from a vector space to itself is called an operator.
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Recall that we defined the notation \(\Lc(V)\) to mean \(\Lc(V{}, V)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) If \(m \ge 2\) and
    \begin{equation*} V = V{}_1 \oplus \dots \oplus V{}_m , \end{equation*}
    where each \(V{}_k\) is a nonzero subspace of \(V{}\text{,}\) then to understand the behavior of \(T\) we only need to understand the behavior of each \(T|_{V{}_k}\text{;}\) here \(T|_{V{}_k}\) denotes the restriction of \(T\) to the smaller domain \(V{}_k\text{.}\) Dealing with \(T|_{V{}_k}\) should be easier than dealing with \(T\) because \(V{}_k\) is a smaller vector space than \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    However, if we intend to apply tools useful in the study of operators (such as taking powers), then we have a problem: \(T|_{V{}_k}\) may not map \(V{}_k\) into itself; in other words, \(T|_{V{}_k}\) may not be an operator on \(V{}_k\text{.}\) Thus we are led to consider only decompositions of \(V\) of the form above in which \(T\) maps each \(V{}_k\) into itself. Hence we now give a name to subspaces of \(V\) that get mapped into themselves by \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 5.2. invariant subspace.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) A subspace \(U\) of \(V\) is called invariant under \(T\) if \(Tu \in U\) for every \(u \in U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus \(U\) is invariant under \(T\) if \(T|_U\) is an operator on \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 5.3. subspace invariant under differentiation operator.

    Suppose that \(T \in \Lc\bigl(\P(\R{})\bigr)\) is defined by \(Tp = p'\!\text{.}\) Then \(\P{}_4(\R{})\text{,}\) which is a subspace of \(\P(\R{})\text{,}\) is invariant under \(T\) because if \(p \in \P(\R{})\) has degree at most \(4\text{,}\) then \(p'\) also has degree at most \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.4. four invariant subspaces, not necessarily all different.

    If \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) then the following subspaces of \(V\) are all invariant under \(T{}\text{.}\)
    1. \(\{ 0 \}\).
      The subspace \(\{0\}\) is invariant under \(T\) because if \(u \in \{0\}\text{,}\) then \(u = 0\) and hence \(Tu = 0 \in \{0\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(V\).
      The subspace \(V\) is invariant under \(T\) because if \(u \in V{}\text{,}\) then \(Tu \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\ker T\).
      The subspace \(\ker T\) is invariant under \(T\) because if \(u \in \ker T\text{,}\) then \(Tu = 0\text{,}\) and hence \(Tu \in \ker T{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\ran T\).
      The subspace \(\ran T\) is invariant under \(T\) because if \(u \in \ran T\text{,}\) then \(Tu \in \ran T{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Must an operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\) have any invariant subspaces other than \(\{0\}\) and \(V\text{?}\) Later we will see that this question has an affirmative answer if \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(\dim V > 1\) (for \(\F{} = \C{}\)) or \(\dim V > 2\) (for \(\F{} = \R{})\text{;}\) see 5.19 and Exercise 5.2.3.29 in Section 5.2.
    🔗
    The previous example noted that \(\ker T\) and \(\ran T\) are invariant under \(T\text{.}\) However, these subspaces do not necessarily provide easy answers to the question above about the existence of invariant subspaces other than \(\{0\}\) and \(V{}\text{,}\) because \(\ker T\) may equal \(\{0\}\) and \(\ran T\) may equal \(V\) (this happens when \(T\) is invertible).
    🔗
    We will return later to a deeper study of invariant subspaces. Now we turn to an investigation of the simplest possible nontrivial invariant subspaces—invariant subspaces of dimension one.
    🔗
    Take any \(v \in V\) with \(v \ne 0\) and let \(U\) equal the set of all scalar multiples of \(v\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} U = \{\lambda v: \lambda \in \F{}\} = \Span (v). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then \(U\) is a one-dimensional subspace of \(V\) (and every one-dimensional subspace of \(V\) is of this form for an appropriate choice of \(v\)). If \(U\) is invariant under an operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) then \(Tv \in U\text{,}\) and hence there is a scalar \(\lambda \in \F{}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} Tv = \lambda v. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Conversely, if \(Tv = \lambda v\) for some \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) then \(\Span (v)\) is a one-dimensional subspace of \(V\) invariant under \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The equation \(Tv = \lambda v\text{,}\) which we have just seen is intimately connected with one-dimensional invariant subspaces, is important enough that the scalars \(\lambda\) and vectors \(v\) satisfying it are given special names.
    🔗

    Definition 5.5. eigenvalue.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) A number \(\lambda \in \F{}\) is called an eigenvalue of \(T\) if there exists \(v \in V\) such that \(v \ne 0\) and \(Tv = \lambda v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The word eigenvalue is half-German, half-English. The German prefix eigen means “own” in the sense of characterizing an intrinsic property.
    🔗
    In the definition above, we require that \(v \ne 0\) because every scalar \(\lambda \in \F{}\) satisfies \(T0 = \lambda 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The comments above show that \(V\) has a one-dimensional subspace invariant under \(T\) if and only if \(T\) has an eigenvalue.
    🔗

    Example 5.6. eigenvalue.

    Define an operator \(T \in \Lc( \F3 )\) by
    \begin{equation*} T(x,y,z) = (7x + 3z, 3x +6y + 9z, -6y) \end{equation*}
    for \((x, y, z) \in \FF3\text{.}\) Then \(T(3,1,-1) = (18, 6, -6) = 6(3,1,-1)\text{.}\) Thus \(6\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The equivalences in the next result, along with many deep results in linear algebra, are valid only in the context of finite-dimensional vector spaces.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.7. equivalent conditions to be an eigenvalue.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional, \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) and \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then the following are equivalent.
    1. \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(T - \lambda I\) is not injective.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(T - \lambda I\) is not surjective.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(T - \lambda I\) is not invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Reminder: \(I \in \Lc(V)\) is the identity operator. Thus \(Iv = v\) for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Proof.
    Conditions (a) and (b) are equivalent because the equation \(Tv = \lambda v\) is equivalent to the equation \((T - \lambda I)v = 0\text{.}\) Conditions (b), (c), and (d) are equivalent by Theorem 3.65.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 5.8. eigenvector.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(\lambda \in \F{}\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) A vector \(v \in V\) is called an eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to \(\lambda\) if \(v \ne 0\) and \(Tv = \lambda v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In other words, a nonzero vector \(v \in V\) is an eigenvector of an operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\) if and only if \(Tv\) is a scalar multiple of \(v\text{.}\) Because \(Tv = \lambda v\) if and only if \((T - \lambda I)v = 0\text{,}\) a vector \(v \in V\) with \(v \ne 0\) is an eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to \(\lambda\) if and only if \(v \in \ker (T - \lambda I)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 5.9. eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc( \F{2} )\) is defined by \(T(w,z) = (-z,w)\text{.}\)
    1. First consider the case \(\F{} = \R{}\text{.}\) Then \(T\) is a counterclockwise rotation by \(90^\circ\) about the origin in \(\RR{2}\text{.}\) An operator has an eigenvalue if and only if there exists a nonzero vector in its domain that gets sent by the operator to a scalar multiple of itself. A \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation of a nonzero vector in \(\R{2}\) cannot equal a scalar multiple of itself. Conclusion: if \(\F{} = \R{}\text{,}\) then \(T\) has no eigenvalues (and thus has no eigenvectors).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Now consider the case \(\F{} = \C{}\text{.}\) To find eigenvalues of \(T\text{,}\) we must find the scalars \(\lambda\) such that \(T(w,z) = \lambda (w, z)\) has some solution other than \(w = z = 0\text{.}\) The equation \(T(w,z) = \lambda (w, z)\) is equivalent to the simultaneous equations
      \begin{equation} -z = \lambda w, w = \lambda z.\tag{5.10} \end{equation}
      Substituting the value for \(w\) given by the second equation into the first equation gives
      \begin{equation*} -z = \lambda^2 z. \end{equation*}
      Now \(z\) cannot equal \(0\) [otherwise (5.10) implies that \(w = 0\text{;}\) we are looking for solutions to (5.10) such that \((w, z)\) is not the \(0\) vector] , so the equation above leads to the equation
      \begin{equation*} -1 = \lambda^2{}. \end{equation*}
      The solutions to this equation are \(\lambda = i\) and \(\lambda = -i\text{.}\) You can verify that \(i\) and \(-i\) are eigenvalues of \(T{}\text{.}\) Indeed, the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue \(i\) are the vectors of the form \((w, -wi)\text{,}\) with \(w \in \C{}\) and \(w \ne 0\text{.}\) Furthermore, the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue \(-i\) are the vectors of the form \((w, wi)\text{,}\) with \(w \in \C{}\) and \(w \ne 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In the next proof, we again use the equivalence
    \begin{equation*} Tv = \lambda v \iff (T - \lambda I)v = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Theorem 5.11. linearly independent eigenvectors.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then every list of eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of \(T\) is linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose the desired result is false. Then there exists a smallest positive integer \(m\) such that there exists a linearly dependent list \(v_1, \ldots, v_m\) of eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_m\) of \(T\) (note that \(m\ge 2\) because an eigenvector is, by definition, nonzero). Thus there exist \(a_1, \ldots, a_m \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) none of which are \(0\) (because of the minimality of \(m\)), such that
    \begin{equation*} a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_m v_m = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Apply \(T - \lambda_m I\) to both sides of the equation above, getting
    \begin{equation*} a_1 (\lambda_1 - \lambda_m) v_1 + \cdots + a_{m-1} (\lambda_{m-1} - \lambda_m) v_{m-1} = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because the eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_m\) are distinct, none of the coefficients above equal \(0\text{.}\) Thus \(v_1, \ldots, v_{m-1}\) is a linearly dependent list of \(m-1\) eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues, contradicting the minimality of \(m\text{.}\) This contradiction completes the proof.
    🔗
    🔗
    The result above leads to a short proof of the result below, which puts an upper bound on the number of distinct eigenvalues that an operator can have.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.12. operator cannot have more eigenvalues than dimension of vector space.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional. Then each operator on \(V\) has at most \(\dim V\) distinct eigenvalues.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Suppose \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m\) are distinct eigenvalues of \(T{}\text{.}\) Let \(v_1, \dots, v_m\) be corresponding eigenvectors. Then 5.11 implies that the list \(v_1, \dots, v_m\) is linearly independent. Thus \(m \le \dim V\) (see 2.22), as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.1.2 Polynomials Applied to Operators

    The main reason that a richer theory exists for operators (which map a vector space into itself) than for more general linear maps is that operators can be raised to powers. In this subsection we define that notion and the concept of applying a polynomial to an operator. This concept will be the key tool that we use in the next section when we prove that every operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space has an eigenvalue.
    🔗
    If \(T\) is an operator, then \(TT\) makes sense (see 3.7) and is also an operator on the same vector space as \(T{}\text{.}\) We usually write \(T^2\) instead of \(TT{}\text{.}\) More generally, we have the following definition of \(T^m\!\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Notation 5.13. \(T^m\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(m\) is a positive integer.
    1. \(T^m \in \Lc(V)\) is defined by \(T^m = \underbrace{T \dotsm T}_{m \textit{ times}}{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(T^0\) is defined to be the identity operator \(I\) on \(V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(T\) is invertible with inverse \(T^{-1}{}\text{,}\) then \(T^{-m} \in \Lc(V)\) is defined by
      \begin{equation*} T^{-m} = ( T^{-1} )^m\!. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    You should verify that if \(T\) is an operator, then
    \begin{equation*} T^m T^n = T^{m+n} \andd ( T^m )^n = T^{mn}\!, \end{equation*}
    where \(m\) and \(n\) are arbitrary integers if \(T\) is invertible and are nonnegative integers if \(T\) is not invertible.
    🔗
    Having defined powers of an operator, we can now define what it means to apply a polynomial to an operator.
    🔗

    Notation 5.14. \(p(T)\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(p \in \P(\F{})\) is a polynomial given by
    \begin{equation*} p(z) = a_0 + a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \dots + a_m z^m \end{equation*}
    for all \(z \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then \(p(T)\) is the operator on \(V\) defined by
    \begin{equation*} p(T) = a_0 I + a_1 T + a_2 T^2 + \dots + a_m T^m\!. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This is a new use of the symbol \(p\) because we are applying \(p\) to operators, not just elements of \(\F{}{}\text{.}\) The idea here is that to evaluate \(p(T)\text{,}\) we simply replace \(z\) with \(T\) in the expression defining \(p\text{.}\) Note that the constant term \(a_0\) in \(p(z)\) becomes the operator \(a_0 I\) \big(which is a reasonable choice because \(a_0 = a_0 z^0\) and thus we should replace \(a_0\) with \(a_0 T^0{}\text{,}\) which equals \(a_0 I\)\big).
    🔗

    Example 5.15. a polynomial applied to the differentiation operator.

    Suppose \(D \in \Lc\bigl(\P(\R{})\bigr)\) is the differentiation operator defined by \(Dq = q'\) and \(p\) is the polynomial defined by \(p(x) = 7 - 3x + 5x^2{}\text{.}\) Then \(p(D) = 7I - 3D + 5D^2{}\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} \bigl(p(D)\bigr)q = 7q - 3q' + 5q'' \end{equation*}
    for every \(q \in \P(\R{})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If we fix an operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) then the function from \(\P(\F{})\) to \(\Lc(V)\) given by \(p \mapsto p(T)\) is linear, as you should verify.
    🔗

    Definition 5.16. product of polynomials.

    If \(p, q \in \P(\F{})\text{,}\) then \(pq \in \P(\F{})\) is the polynomial defined by
    \begin{equation*} (pq)(z) = p(z) q(z) \end{equation*}
    for all \(z \in \F{}{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The order does not matter in taking products of polynomials of a single operator, as shown by (b) in the next result.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.17. multiplicative properties.

    Suppose \(p, q \in \P(\F{})\) and \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then
    1. \((pq)(T) = p(T)q(T)\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(p(T)q(T) = q(T)p(T)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Informal proof: When a product of polynomials is expanded using the distributive property, it does not matter whether the symbol is \(z\) or \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Suppose \(\displaystyle p(z) = \sum_{j=1[=0]}^m a_j z^j\) and \(\displaystyle q(z) = \sum_{k=0}^n b_k z^k\) for all \(z \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} (pq)(z) = \sum_{j=1[=0]}^m \sum_{k=0}^n a_j b_k z^{j+k}\!. \end{equation*}
      Thus
      \begin{align*} (pq)(T) \amp = \sum_{j=1[=0]}^m \sum_{k=0}^n a_j b_k T^{j+k}\\ \amp = ( \sum_{j=1[=0]}^m a_j T^j ) ( \sum_{k=0}^n b_k T^k )\\ \amp = p(T) q(T)\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Using (a) twice, we have \(p(T)q(T) = (pq)(T) = (qp)(T) = q(T)p(T)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    We observed earlier that if \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) then the subspaces \(\ker T\) and \(\ran T\) are invariant under \(T\) (see 5.4). Now we show that the null space and the range of every polynomial of \(T\) are also invariant under \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 5.18. null space and range of \(p(T)\) are invariant under \(T\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(p \in \P(\F{})\text{.}\) Then \(\ker p(T)\) and \(\ran p(T)\) are invariant under \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(u \in \ker p(T)\text{.}\) Then \(p(T)u = 0\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} \bigl(p(T)\bigr)(Tu) = ( p(T) T )(u) = ( T p(T) )(u) = T\bigl(p(T)u\bigr) = T(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence \(Tu \in \ker p(T)\text{.}\) Thus \(\ker p(T)\) is invariant under \(T\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    Suppose \(u \in \ran p(T)\text{.}\) Then there exists \(v \in V\) such that \(u = p(T)v\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} Tu = T\bigl(p(T)v\bigr) = p(T)(Tv). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence \(Tu \in \ran p(T)\text{.}\) Thus \(\ran p(T)\) is invariant under \(T\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
