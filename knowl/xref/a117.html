Example 5.9. eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Suppose \(T \in \Lc( \F{2} )\) is defined by \(T(w,z) = (-z,w)\text{.}\)
  1. First consider the case \(\F{} = \R{}\text{.}\) Then \(T\) is a counterclockwise rotation by \(90^\circ\) about the origin in \(\RR{2}\text{.}\) An operator has an eigenvalue if and only if there exists a nonzero vector in its domain that gets sent by the operator to a scalar multiple of itself. A \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation of a nonzero vector in \(\R{2}\) cannot equal a scalar multiple of itself. Conclusion: if \(\F{} = \R{}\text{,}\) then \(T\) has no eigenvalues (and thus has no eigenvectors).
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Now consider the case \(\F{} = \C{}\text{.}\) To find eigenvalues of \(T\text{,}\) we must find the scalars \(\lambda\) such that \(T(w,z) = \lambda (w, z)\) has some solution other than \(w = z = 0\text{.}\) The equation \(T(w,z) = \lambda (w, z)\) is equivalent to the simultaneous equations
    \begin{equation} -z = \lambda w, w = \lambda z.\tag{5.1} \end{equation}
    Substituting the value for \(w\) given by the second equation into the first equation gives
    \begin{equation*} -z = \lambda^2 z. \end{equation*}
    Now \(z\) cannot equal \(0\) [otherwise (5.1) implies that \(w = 0\text{;}\) we are looking for solutions to (5.1) such that \((w, z)\) is not the \(0\) vector] , so the equation above leads to the equation
    \begin{equation*} -1 = \lambda^2{}. \end{equation*}
    The solutions to this equation are \(\lambda = i\) and \(\lambda = -i\text{.}\) You can verify that \(i\) and \(-i\) are eigenvalues of \(T{}\text{.}\) Indeed, the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue \(i\) are the vectors of the form \((w, -wi)\text{,}\) with \(w \in \C{}\) and \(w \ne 0\text{.}\) Furthermore, the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue \(-i\) are the vectors of the form \((w, wi)\text{,}\) with \(w \in \C{}\) and \(w \ne 0\text{.}\)
