    Section 6.3 Orthogonal Complements and Minimization Problems

    Subsection 6.3.1 Orthogonal Complements

    Definition 6.46. orthogonal complement, \(U^\perp\).

    If \(U\) is a subset of \(V{}\text{,}\) then the orthogonal complement of \(U\text{,}\) denoted by \(U^\perp{}\text{,}\) is the set of all vectors in \(V\) that are orthogonal to every vector in \(U\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} U^\perp = \{ v \in V: \langle u,v \rangle = 0 \ for every\ u \in U \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The orthogonal complement \(U^\perp\) depends on \(V\) as well as on \(U\text{.}\) However, the inner product space \(V\) should always be clear from the context and thus it can be omitted from the notation.
    🔗

    Example 6.47. orthogonal complements.

    1. If \(V = \R3\) and \(U\) is the subset of \(V\) consisting of the single point \((2,3,5)\text{,}\) then \(U^\perp\) is the plane \(\{ (x,y,z) \in \R3: 2x+3y+5z = 0 \}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(V = \R3\) and \(U\) is the plane \(\{ (x,y,z) \in \R3: 2x+3y+5z = 0 \}\text{,}\) then \(U^\perp\) is the line \(\{ (2t, 3t, 5t): t \in \R{} \}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. More generally, if \(U\) is a plane in \(\R3\) containing the origin, then \(U^\perp\) is the line containing the origin that is perpendicular to \(U\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If \(U\) is a line in \(\R3\) containing the origin, then \(U^\perp\) is the plane containing the origin that is perpendicular to \(U\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. If \(V = \F5\) and \(U = \{ (a,b, 0, 0, 0) \in \F5: a,b \in \F{} \}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} U^\perp = \{ (0, 0, x,y,z) \in \F5: x,y,z \in \F{} \}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    6. If \(e_1, \ldots, e_m, f_1, \ldots, f_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V{}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} ( \Span( e_1, \ldots, e_m) )^\perp = \Span( f_1, \ldots, f_n). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We begin with some straightforward consequences of the definition.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.48. properties of orthogonal complement.

    1. If \(U\) is a subset of \(V{}\text{,}\) then \(U^\perp\) is a subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\{0\}^\perp = V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(V^\perp = \{0\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If \(U\) is a subset of \(V{}\text{,}\) then \(U \cap U^\perp \subset \{0\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. If \(G\) and \(H\) are subsets of \(V\) and \(G \subset H\text{,}\) then \(H^\perp \subset G^\perp{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Suppose \(U\) is a subset of \(V{}\text{.}\) Then \(\langle u,0 \rangle = 0\) for every \(u \in U\text{;}\) thus \(0 \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Suppose \(v, w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) If \(u \in U\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \langle u,v+w \rangle = \langle u,v \rangle + \langle u,w \rangle = 0 + 0 = 0. \end{equation*}
      Thus \(v + w \in U^\perp{}\text{,}\) which shows that \(U^\perp\) is closed under addition. Similarly, suppose \(\lambda \in \F{}\) and \(v \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) If \(u \in U\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \langle u, \lambda v \rangle = \overline{\lambda} \langle u,v \rangle = \overline{\lambda} \cdot 0 = 0. \end{equation*}
      Thus \(\lambda v \in U^\perp{}\text{,}\) which shows that \(U^\perp\) is closed under scalar multiplication. Thus \(U^\perp\) is a subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose that \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then \(\langle 0,v \rangle = 0\text{,}\) which implies that \(v \in \{0\}^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(\{0\}^\perp = V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Suppose that \(v \in V^\perp{}\text{.}\) Then \(\langle v,v \rangle = 0\text{,}\) which implies that \(v = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(V^\perp = \{0\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Suppose \(U\) is a subset of \(V\) and \(u \in U \cap U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Then \(\langle u,u \rangle = 0\text{,}\) which implies that \(u = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(U \cap U^\perp \subset \{0\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Suppose \(G\) and \(H\) are subsets of \(V\) and \(G \subset H\text{.}\) Suppose \(v \in H^\perp{}\text{.}\) Then \(\langle u,v \rangle = 0\) for every \(u \in H\text{,}\) which implies that \(\langle u,v \rangle = 0\) for every \(u \in G\text{.}\) Hence \(v \in G^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(H^\perp \subset G^\perp{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall that if \(U\) and \(W\) are subspaces of \(V{}\text{,}\) then \(V\) is the direct sum of \(U\) and \(W\) (written \(V = U \oplus W\)) if each element of \(V\) can be written in exactly one way as a vector in \(U\) plus a vector in \(W\) (see 1.41). Furthermore, this happens if and only if \(V = U + W\) and \(U \cap W = \{ 0 \}\) (see 1.46).
    🔗
    The next result shows that every finite-dimensional subspace of \(V\) leads to a natural direct sum decomposition of \(V{}\text{.}\) See Exercise 6.3.3.16 for an example showing that the result below can fail without the hypothesis that the subspace \(U\) is finite-dimensional.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.49. direct sum of a subspace and its orthogonal complement.

    Suppose \(U\) is a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} V = U \oplus U^\perp{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First we will show that
    \begin{equation*} V = U + U^\perp{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To do this, suppose that \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Let \(e_1, \dots, e_m\) be an orthonormal basis of \(U\text{.}\) We want to write \(v\) as the sum of a vector in \(U\) and a vector orthogonal to \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We have
    \begin{equation} v = \underbrace{\langle v, e_1 \rangle e_1 + \dots + \langle v, e_m \rangle e_m}_u + \underbrace{v - \langle v, e_1 \rangle e_1 - \dots - \langle v, e_m \rangle e_m}_w\!.\tag{6.50} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Let \(u\) and \(w\) be defined as in the equation above (as was done in the proof of Theorem 6.26). Because each \(e_k \in U\text{,}\) we see that \(u \in U\text{.}\) Because \(e_1, \dots, e_m\) is an orthonormal list, for each \(k = 1, \dots, m\) we have
    \begin{align*} \langle w, e_k \rangle \amp = \langle v, e_k \rangle - \langle v, e_k \rangle\\ \amp = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus \(w\) is orthogonal to every vector in \(\Span(e_1, \dots, e_m)\text{,}\) which shows that \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Hence we have written \(v = u + w\text{,}\) where \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{,}\) completing the proof that \(V = U + U^\perp{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    From 6.48(d), we know that \(U \cap U^\perp = \{0\}\text{.}\) Now equation \(V = U + U^\perp{}\) implies that \(V = U \oplus U^\perp\) (see 1.46).
    🔗
    🔗
    Now we can see how to compute \(\dim U^\perp\) from \(\dim U\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 6.51. dimension of orthogonal complement.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(U\) is a subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \dim U^\perp = \dim V - \dim U. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    The formula for \(\dim U^\perp\) follows immediately from Theorem 6.49 and Theorem 3.94.
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result is an important consequence of Theorem 6.49.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.52. orthogonal complement of the orthogonal complement.

    Suppose \(U\) is a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} U = ( U^\perp )^\perp{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First we will show that
    \begin{equation} U \subset ( U^\perp )^\perp{}.\tag{6.53} \end{equation}
    🔗
    To do this, suppose \(u \in U\text{.}\) Then \(\langle u, w \rangle = 0\) for every \(w \in U^\perp\) \big(by the definition of \(U^\perp\)\big). Because \(u\) is orthogonal to every vector in \(U^\perp{}\text{,}\) we have \(u \in ( U^\perp )^\perp{}\text{,}\) completing the proof of (6.53).
    🔗
    To prove the inclusion in the other direction, suppose \(v \in ( U^\perp )^\perp{}\text{.}\) By 6.49, we can write \(v = u + w\text{,}\) where \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) We have \(v - u = w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Because \(v \in ( U^\perp )^\perp\) and \(u \in ( U^\perp )^\perp\) (from (6.53)), we have \(v - u \in ( U^\perp )^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(v - u \in U^\perp \cap ( U^\perp )^\perp{}\text{,}\) which implies that \(v - u = 0\) [by Theorem 6.48(d)], which implies that \(v = u\text{,}\) which implies that \(v \in U\text{.}\) Thus \(( U^\perp )^\perp \subset U\text{,}\) which along with (6.53) completes the proof.
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Exercise 6.3.3.16 (a) shows that the result below is not true without the hypothesis that \(U\) is finite-dimensional.
    🔗
    Suppose \(U\) is a subspace of \(V\) and we want to show that \(U = V{}\text{.}\) In some situations, the easiest way to do this is to show that the only vector orthogonal to \(U\) is \(0\text{,}\) and then use the result below. For example, the result below is useful for Exercise 6.3.3.4.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.54. \(U^\perp = \{0\} \iff U = V\) (for \(U\) a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V\)).

    Suppose \(U\) is a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} U^\perp = \{0\} \iff U = V{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First suppose \(U^\perp = \{ 0 \}\text{.}\) Then by Theorem 6.52, \(U = ( U^\perp )^\perp = \{ 0 \}^\perp= V{}\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    Conversely, if \(U = V{}\text{,}\) then \(U^\perp = V^\perp = \{ 0 \}\) by Theorem 6.48(c).
    🔗
    🔗
    We now define an operator \(P_{U}\) for each finite-dimensional subspace \(U\) of \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 6.55. orthogonal projection, \(P_{U}\).

    Suppose \(U\) is a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\) The orthogonal projection of \(V\) onto \(U\) is the operator \(P_{U} \in \Lc(V)\) defined as follows: For each \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) write \(v = u + w\text{,}\) where \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Then let \(P_{U} v = u\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The direct sum decomposition \(V = U \oplus U^\perp\) given by Theorem 6.49 shows that each \(v \in V\) can be uniquely written in the form \(v = u + w\) with \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(P_{U} v\) is well defined. See the figure that accompanies the proof of Theorem 6.61 for the picture describing \(P_{U} v\) that you should keep in mind.
    🔗

    Example 6.56. orthogonal projection onto one-dimensional subspace.

    Suppose \(u \in V\) with \(u \ne 0\) and \(U\) is the one-dimensional subspace of \(V\) defined by \(U = \Span(u)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} v = \frac{\langle v, u \rangle }{\|u\|^2} u + ( v - \frac{\langle v, u \rangle }{\|u\|^2} u ), \end{equation*}
    where the first term on the right is in \(\Span(u)\) (and thus is in \(U\)) and the second term on the right is orthogonal to \(u\) (and thus is in \(U^\perp)\text{.}\) Thus \(P_{U} v\) equals the first term on the right. In other words, we have the formula
    \begin{equation*} P_{U} v = \frac{\langle v, u \rangle }{\|u\|^2} u \end{equation*}
    for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The formula above becomes \(P_{U} u = u\) if \(v = u\) and becomes \(P_{U} v = 0\) if \(v \in \{ u \}^\perp{}\text{.}\) These equations are special cases of (b) and (c) in the next result.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 6.57. properties of orthogonal projection \(P_{U}\).

    Suppose \(U\) is a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\) Then
    1. \(P_{U} \in \Lc(V)\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(P_{U} u = u\) for every \(u \in U\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(P_{U} w = 0\) for every \(w \in U^\perp\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\ran P_{U} = U\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\ker P_{U} = U^\perp\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. \(v - P_{U} v \in U^\perp\) for every \(v \in V\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. \({P_{U}}^{2} = P_{U}\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    8. \(\|P_{U} v\| \le \|v\|\) for every \(v \in V\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    9. if \(e_1, \dots, e_m\) is an orthonormal basis of \(U\) and \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} P_{U} v = \langle v, e_1 \rangle e_1 + \dots + \langle v, e_m \rangle e_m. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. To show that \(P_{U}\) is a linear map on \(V{}\text{,}\) suppose \(v_1, v_2 \in V{}\text{.}\) Write
      \begin{equation*} v_1 = u_1 + w_1 \andd v_2 = u_2 + w_2 \end{equation*}
      with \(u_1, u_2 \in U\) and \(w_1, w_2 \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(P_{U} v_1 = u_1\) and \(P_{U} v_2 = u_2\text{.}\) Now
      \begin{equation*} v_1 + v_2 = (u_1 + u_2) + (w_1 + w_2), \end{equation*}
      where \(u_1 + u_2 \in U\) and \(w_1 + w_2 \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus
      \begin{equation*} P_{U}(v_1 + v_2) = u_1 + u_2 = P_{U} v_1 + P_{U} v_2. \end{equation*}
      Similarly, suppose \(\lambda \in \F{}\) and \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Write \(v = u + w\text{,}\) where \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Then \(\lambda v = \lambda u + \lambda w\) with \(\lambda u \in U\) and \(\lambda w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(P_{U}(\lambda v) = \lambda u = \lambda P_{U}v\text{.}\) Hence \(P_{U}\) is a linear map from \(V\) to \(V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(u \in U\text{.}\) We can write \(u = u + 0\text{,}\) where \(u \in U\) and \(0 \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(P_{U}u = u\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Suppose \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) We can write \(w = 0 + w\text{,}\) where \(0 \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(P_{U}w = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The definition of \(P_{U}\) implies that \(\ran P_{U} \subset U\text{.}\) Furthermore, (b) implies that \(U \subset \ran P_{U}\text{.}\) Thus \(\ran P_{U} = U\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. The inclusion \(U^\perp \subset \ker P_{U}\) follows from (c). To prove the inclusion in the other direction, note that if \(v \in \ker P_{U}\) then the decomposition given by Theorem 6.49 must be \(v = 0 + v\text{,}\) where \(0 \in U\) and \(v \in U^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus \(\ker P_{U} \subset U^\perp{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. If \(v \in V\) and \(v = u + w\) with \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} v - P_{U}v = v - u = w \in U^\perp{}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    7. If \(v \in V\) and \(v = u + w\) with \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} ( P_{U}^{2} )v = P_{U}(P_{U} v) = P_{U} u = u = P_{U}v. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    8. If \(v \in V\) and \(v = u + w\) with \(u \in U\) and \(w \in U^\perp{}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \|P_{U} v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 \le \|u\|^2 + \|w\|^2 = \|v\|^2{}, \end{equation*}
      where the last equality comes from the Pythagorean theorem.
      🔗
      🔗
    9. The formula for \(P_{U} v\) follows from equation (6.50) in the proof of Theorem 6.49.
      🔗
      🔗

    In the previous section we proved the Riesz representation theorem Theorem 6.42, whose key part states that every linear functional on a finite-dimensional inner product space is given by taking the inner product with some fixed vector. Seeing a different proof often provides new insight. Thus we now give a new proof of the key part of the Riesz representation theorem using orthogonal complements instead of orthonormal bases as in our previous proof.
    🔗
    The restatement below of the Riesz representation theorem provides an identification of \(V\) with \(V'\!\text{.}\) We will prove only the “onto” part of the result below because the more routine “one-to-one” part of the result can be proved as in Theorem 6.42.
    🔗
    Intuition behind this new proof: If \(\phi \in V'\!\text{,}\) \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) and \(\phi(u) = \langle u,v \rangle\) for all \(u \in V{}\text{,}\) then \(v \in (\ker \phi)^\perp{}\text{.}\) However, \((\ker \phi)^\perp\) is a one-dimensional subspace of \(V\) (except for the trivial case in which \(\phi = 0\)), as follows from Theorem 6.51 and Theorem 3.21. Thus we can obtain \(v\) by choosing any nonzero element of \((\ker \phi)^\perp\) and then multiplying by an appropriate scalar, as is done in the proof below.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.58. Riesz representation theorem, revisited.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional. For each \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) define \(\phi_v \in V'\) by
    \begin{equation*} \phi_v(u) = \langle u, v \rangle \end{equation*}
    for each \(u \in V{}\text{.}\) Then \(v \mapsto \phi_v\) is a one-to-one function from \(V\) onto \(V'\!\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Caution: The function \(v \mapsto \phi_v\) is a linear mapping from \(V\) to \(V'\) if \(\F{} = \R{}\text{.}\) However, this function is not linear if \(\F{}=\C{}\) because \(\phi_{\lambda v} = \bar{\lambda} \phi_v\) if \(\lambda \in \C{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Proof.
    To show that \(v \mapsto \phi_v\) is surjective, suppose \(\phi \in V'\!\text{.}\) If \(\phi = 0\text{,}\) then \(\phi = \phi_0\text{.}\) Thus assume \(\phi \ne 0\text{.}\) Hence \(\ker \phi \ne V{}\text{,}\) which implies that \((\ker \phi)^\perp \ne \{ 0 \}\) (by Theorem 6.49 with \(U = \ker \phi\)).
    🔗
    Let \(w \in (\ker \phi)^\perp\) be such that \(w \ne 0\text{.}\) Let
    \begin{equation} v = \frac{ \overline{\phi(w)} }{\lVert w \rVert ^2} w.\tag{6.59} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Then \(v \in (\ker \phi)^\perp{}\text{.}\) Also, \(v \ne 0\) (because \(w \notin \ker \phi\)).
    🔗
    Taking the norm of both sides of (6.59) gives
    \begin{equation} \lVert v \rVert = \frac{ \lvert \phi(w) \rvert }{\lVert w \rVert }.\tag{6.60} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Applying \(\phi\) to both sides of (6.59) and then using (6.60), we have
    \begin{equation*} \phi(v) = \frac{ \lvert \phi(w) \rvert ^2 }{ \lVert w \rVert ^2} = \lVert v \rVert ^2{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now suppose \(u \in V{}\text{.}\) Using the equation above, we have
    \begin{equation*} u = ( u - \frac{ \phi(u) }{ \phi(v) } v ) + \frac{ \phi(u) }{ \lVert v \rVert ^2 } v. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The first term in parentheses above is in \(\ker \phi\) and hence is orthogonal to \(v\text{.}\) Thus taking the inner product of both sides of the equation above with \(v\) shows that
    \begin{equation*} \langle u, v \rangle = \frac{ \phi(u) }{ \lVert v \rVert ^2 } \langle v, v \rangle = \phi(u). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(\phi = \phi_v\text{,}\) showing that \(v \mapsto \phi_v\) is surjective, as desired.

    Subsection 6.3.2 Minimization Problems

    Aside

    🔗
    The following problem often arises: Given a subspace \(U\) of \(V\) and a point \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) find a point \(u \in U\) such that \(\|v - u\|\) is as small as possible. The next result shows that \(u = P_{U} v\) is the unique solution of this minimization problem.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.61. minimizing distance to a subspace.

    Suppose \(U\) is a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V{}\text{,}\) \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) and \(u \in U\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \| v - P_{U} v \| \le \| v - u \|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Furthermore, the inequality above is an equality if and only if \(u = P_{U} v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We have
    \begin{align} \| v - P_{U} v \|^2 \amp \le \|v - P_{U} v\|^2 + \|P_{U} v - u\|^2\tag{6.62}\\ \amp = \lVert (v - P_{U} v) + (P_{U} v - u) \rVert ^2\notag\\ \amp = \| v - u \|^2{}\text{,}\notag \end{align}
    🔗
    
    Missing description
    🔗
    \(P_{U} v\) is the closest point in \(U\) to \(v\text{.}\)
    🔗

Taking square roots gives the desired inequality.
    🔗

    Example 6.63. using linear algebra to approximate the sine function.

    Suppose we want to find a polynomial \(u\) with real coefficients and of degree at most \(5\) that approximates the sine function as well as possible on the interval \([-\pi, \pi]\text{,}\) in the sense that
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \lvert \sin x - u(x) \rvert ^2 dx \end{equation*}
    is as small as possible.
    🔗

    \begin{equation} \langle f, g \rangle = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} fg.\tag{6.64} \end{equation}
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} \text{ Find \(u\in U\) such that \(\|v - u\|\) is as small as possible. } \end{equation*}

    Subsection 6.3.3 Pseudoinverse

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\) and \(w \in W{}\text{.}\) Consider the problem of finding \(v \in V\) such that
    \begin{equation*} Tv = w. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example, if \(V = \F n\) and \(W = \FF m\text{,}\) then the equation above could represent a system of \(m\) linear equations in \(n\) unknowns \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\text{,}\) where \(v = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(T\) is invertible, then the unique solution to the equation above is \(v = T^{-1}w\text{.}\) However, if \(T\) is not invertible, then for some \(w \in W\) there may not exist any solutions of the equation above, and for some \(w \in W\) there may exist infinitely many solutions of the equation above.
    🔗
    If \(T\) is not invertible, then we can still try to do as well as possible with the equation above. For example, if the equation above has no solutions, then instead of solving the equation \(Tv - w = 0\text{,}\) we can try to find \(v \in V\) such that \(\lVert Tv - w \rVert\) is as small as possible. As another example, if the equation above has infinitely many solutions \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then among all those solutions we can try to find one such that \(\lVert v \rVert\) is as small as possible.
    🔗
    The pseudoinverse will provide the tool to solve the equation above as well as possible, even when \(T\) is not invertible. We need the next result to define the pseudoinverse.
    🔗
    In the next two proofs, we will use without further comment the result that if \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{,}\) then \(\ker T\text{,}\) \((\ker T)^\perp{}\text{,}\) and \(\ran T\) are all finite-dimensional.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.67. restriction of a linear map to obtain a one-to-one and onto map.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then \(T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}\) is an injective map of \((\ker T)^\perp\) onto \(\ran T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose that \(v \in (\ker T)^\perp\) and \(T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}v = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(Tv = 0\) and thus \(v \in (\ker T) \cap (\ker T)^\perp{}\text{,}\) which implies that \(v = 0\) [by Theorem 6.48(d)] . Hence \(\ker T|_{(\ker T)^\perp} = \{ 0 \}\text{,}\) which implies that \(T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}\) is injective, as desired.
    🔗
    Clearly \(\ran T|_{(\ker T)^\perp} \subset \ran T{}\text{.}\) To prove the inclusion in the other direction, suppose \(w \in \ran T{}\text{.}\) Hence there exists \(v \in V\) such that \(w = Tv\text{.}\) There exist \(u \in \ker T\) and \(x \in (\ker T)^\perp\) such that \(v = u + x\) (by Theorem 6.49). Now
    \begin{equation*} T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}x = Tx = Tv - Tu = w - 0 = w, \end{equation*}
    which shows that \(w \in \ran T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}\text{.}\) Hence \(\ran T \subset \ran T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}\text{,}\) completing the proof that \(\ran T|_{(\ker T)^\perp} = \ran T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    To produce the pseudoinverse notation \(T^\dagger\) in \TeX, type T\^ \backslash dagger.
    🔗
    Now we can define the pseudoinverse \(T^\dagger\) (pronounced “\(T\) dagger” ) of a linear map \(T{}\text{.}\) In the next definition (and from now on), think of \(T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}\) as an invertible linear map from \((\ker T)^\perp\) onto \(\ran T\text{,}\) as is justified by the result above.
    🔗

    Definition 6.68. pseudoinverse, \(T^\dagger\).

    Suppose that \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) The pseudoinverse \(T^\dagger \in \Lc(W{}, V)\) of \(T\) is the linear map from \(W\) to \(V\) defined by
    \begin{equation*} T^\dagger w = (T|_{(\ker T)^\perp})^{-1} P_{\!\ran T} w \end{equation*}
    for each \(w \in W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall that \(P_{\!\ran T} w = 0\) if \(w \in (\ran T)^\perp\) and \(P_{\!\ran T} w = w\) if \(w \in \ran T{}\text{.}\) Thus if \(w \in (\ran T)^\perp{}\text{,}\) then \(T^\dagger w = 0\text{,}\) and if \(w \in \ran T\text{,}\) then \(T^\dagger w\) is the unique element of \((\ker T)^\perp\) such that \(T ( T^\dagger w ) = w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The pseudoinverse behaves much like an inverse, as we will see.
    🔗

    Theorem 6.69. algebraic properties of the pseudoinverse.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{.}\)
    1. If \(T\) is invertible, then \(T^\dagger = T^{-1}{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(TT^\dagger = P_{\!\ran T} =\) the orthogonal projection of \(W\) onto \(\ran T{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(T^\dagger T = P_{\!(\ker T)^\perp} =\) the orthogonal projection of \(V\) onto \((\ker T)^\perp{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Suppose \(T\) is invertible. Then \((\ker T)^\perp = V\) and \(\ran T = W{}\text{.}\) Thus \(T|_{(\ker T)^\perp}= T\) and \(P_{\!\ran T}\) is the identity operator on \(W{}\text{.}\) Hence \(T^\dagger = T^{-1}{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(w \in \ran T{}\text{.}\) Thus
      \begin{equation*} T T^\dagger w = T (T|_{(\ker T)^\perp})^{-1} w = w = P_{\!\ran T} w. \end{equation*}
      If \(w \in (\ran T)^\perp{}\text{,}\) then \(T^\dagger w = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(TT^\dagger w = 0 = P_{\!\ran T} w\text{.}\) Thus \(TT^\dagger\) and \(P_{\!\ran T}\) agree on \(\ran T\) and on \((\ran T)^\perp{}\text{.}\) Hence these two linear maps are equal (by Theorem 6.49).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Suppose \(v \in (\ker T)^\perp{}\text{.}\) Because \(Tv \in \ran T{}\text{,}\) the definition of \(T^\dagger\) shows that
      \begin{equation*} T^\dagger(Tv)= (T|_{(\ker T)^\perp})^{-1} (Tv) = v = P_{\!(\ker T)^\perp} v. \end{equation*}
      If \(v \in \ker T\text{,}\) then \(T^\dagger T v = 0 = P_{\!(\ker T)^\perp} v\text{.}\) Thus \(T^\dagger T\) and \(P_{\!(\ker T)^\perp}\) agree on \((\ker T)^\perp\) and on \(\ker T{}\text{.}\) Hence these two linear maps are equal (by Theorem 6.49).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The pseudoinverse is also called the Moore–Penrose inverse.
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose that \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) If \(T\) is surjective, then \(TT^\dagger\) is the identity operator on \(W{}\text{,}\) as follows from (b) in the result above. If \(T\) is injective, then \(T^\dagger T\) is the identity operator on \(V{}\text{,}\) as follows from (c) in the result above. For additional algebraic properties of the pseudoinverse, see Exercises 6.3.3.19– Exercise 6.3.3.23.
    🔗
    For \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\) and \(w \in W{}\text{,}\) we now return to the problem of finding \(v \in V\) that solves the equation
    \begin{equation*} Tv = w. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As we noted earlier, if \(T\) is invertible, then \(v = T^{-1}w\) is the unique solution, but if \(T\) is not invertible, then \(T^{-1}\) is not defined. However, the pseudoinverse \(T^\dagger\) is defined. Taking \(v = T^\dagger w\) makes \(Tv\) as close to \(w\) as possible, as shown by (a) of the next result. Thus the pseudoinverse provides what is called a best fit to the equation above.
    🔗
    Among all vectors \(v \in V\) that make \(Tv\) as close as possible to \(w\text{,}\) the vector \(T^\dagger w\) has the smallest norm, as shown by combining (b) in the next result with the condition for equality in (a).
    🔗

    Theorem 6.70. pseudoinverse provides best approximate solution or best solution.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional, \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{,}\) and \(w \in W{}\text{.}\)
    1. If \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \lVert T(T^\dagger w) - w \rVert \le \lVert Tv - w \rVert , \end{equation*}
      with equality if and only if \(v \in T^\dagger w + \ker T{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(v \in T^\dagger w + \ker T\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \lVert T^\dagger w \rVert \le \lVert v \rVert , \end{equation*}
      with equality if and only if \(v = T^\dagger w\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Suppose \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} Tv - w = (Tv - TT^\dagger w) + (TT^\dagger w - w). \end{equation*}
      The first term in parentheses above is in \(\ran T{}\text{.}\) Because the operator \(TT^\dagger\) is the orthogonal projection of \(W\) onto \(\ran T\) [by Theorem 6.69(b)] , the second term in parentheses above is in \((\ran T)^\perp\) [see 6.57(f)] . Thus the Pythagorean theorem implies the desired inequality that the norm of the second term in parentheses above is less than or equal to \(\lVert Tv - w \rVert\text{,}\) with equality if and only if the first term in parentheses above equals \(0\text{.}\) Hence we have equality if and only if \(v - T^\dagger w \in \ker T\text{,}\) which is equivalent to the statement that \(v \in T^\dagger w + \ker T\text{,}\) completing the proof of (a).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(v \in T^\dagger w + \ker T{}\text{.}\) Hence \(v - T^\dagger w \in \ker T{}\text{.}\) Now
      \begin{equation*} v = (v - T^\dagger w) + T^\dagger w. \end{equation*}
      The definition of \(T^\dagger\) implies that \(T^\dagger w \in (\ker T)^\perp{}\text{.}\) Thus the Pythagorean theorem implies that \(\lVert T^\dagger w \rVert \le \lVert v \rVert\text{,}\) with equality if and only if \(v = T^\dagger w\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    A formula for \(T^\dagger\) will be given in the next chapter (see (7.78)).
    🔗

    Example 6.71. pseudoinverse of a linear map from \(\F4\) to \(\F3\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc( \F4, \F3 )\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} T(a, b, c, d) = (a + b + c, 2c + d, 0). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This linear map is neither injective nor surjective, but we can compute its pseudoinverse. To do this, first note that \(\ran T = \{ (x,y,0) : x, y \in \F{} \}\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} P_{\ran T}(x,y,z) = (x, y, 0) \end{equation*}
    for each \((x, y, z) \in \FF3\text{.}\) Also,
    \begin{equation*} \ker T = \{ (a, b, c, d) \in \F4 : a+b+c= 0 \text{ and } 2c + d = 0 \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The list \((-1, 1, 0, 0), (-1, 0, 1, -2)\) of two vectors in \(\ker T\) spans \(\ker T\) because if \((a, b, c, d) \in \ker T\) then
    \begin{equation*} (a, b, c, d) = b(-1, 1, 0, 0) + c(-1,0,1,-2). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because the list \((-1, 1, 0, 0), (-1, 0, 1, -2)\) is linearly independent, this list is a basis of \(\ker T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now suppose \((x, y, z) \in \FF3\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation} T^\dagger(x, y, z) = (T|_{(\ker T)^\perp})^{-1} P_{\!\ran T} (x, y, z) = (T|_{(\ker T)^\perp})^{-1} (x, y, 0).\tag{6.72} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The right side of the equation above is the vector \((a, b, c, d) \in \F4\) such that \(T(a, b, c, d) = (x, y, 0)\) and \((a, b, c, d) \in (\ker T)^\perp{}\text{.}\) In other words, \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) \(d\) must satisfy the following equations:
    \begin{align*} a + b + c \amp =x\\ 2c + d \amp = y\\ -a + b \amp = 0\\ -a + c - 2d \amp = 0\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the first two equations are equivalent to the equation \(T(a, b, c, d) = (x, y, 0)\) and the last two equations come from the condition for \((a, b, c, d)\) to be orthogonal to each of the basis vectors \((-1, 1, 0, 0), (-1, 0, 1, -2)\) in this basis of \(\ker T{}\text{.}\) Thinking of \(x\) and \(y\) as constants and \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\text{,}\) \(d\) as unknowns, we can solve the system above of four equations in four unknowns, getting
    \begin{equation*} a = \tfrac{1}{11} (5x-2y),\ b = \tfrac{1}{11} (5x-2y),\ c = \tfrac{1}{11} (x+4y),\ d = \tfrac{1}{11} (-2x + 3y). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence (6.72) tells us that
    \begin{equation*} T^\dagger(x,y,z) = \tfrac{1}{11} (5x-2y, 5x-2y, x+4y, -2x +3y). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The formula above for \(T^\dagger\) shows that \(TT^\dagger(x, y, z) = (x, y, 0)\) for all \((x, y, z) \in \FF3\text{,}\) which illustrates the equation \(TT^\dagger = P_{\!\ran T}\) from Theorem 6.69(b).

    \begin{equation*} \{v_1, \dots, v_m\}^\perp = \bigl( \Span(v_1, \dots, v_m) \bigr)^\perp{}. \end{equation*}

    2.

    Suppose \(U\) is a subspace of \(V\) with basis \(u_1, \dots, u_m\) and
    \begin{equation*} u_1, \dots, u_m, v_1, \dots, v_n \end{equation*}
    is a basis of \(V{}\text{.}\) Prove that if the Gram–Schmidt procedure is applied to the basis of \(V\) above, producing a list \(e_1, \dots, e_m, f_1, \dots, f_n\text{,}\) then \(e_1, \dots, e_m\) is an orthonormal basis of \(U\) and \(f_1, \dots, f_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(U^\perp{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Suppose \(U\) is the subspace of \(\R4\) defined by
    \begin{equation*} U = \Span\bigl( (1,2,3,-4), (-5,4, 3, 2) \bigr). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Find an orthonormal basis of \(U\) and an orthonormal basis of \(U^\perp{}\text{.}\)

    4.

    Suppose \(e_1, \ldots, e_n\) is a list of vectors in \(V\) with \(\lVert e_k \rVert = 1\) for each \(k=1, \ldots, n\) and
    \begin{equation*} \|v\|^2 = \lvert \langle v, e_1 \rangle \rvert ^2 + \dots + \lvert \langle v, e_n \rangle \rvert ^2 \end{equation*}
    for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Prove that \(e_1, \ldots, e_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V{}\text{.}\) Note: This exercise provides a converse to Theorem 6.30 (b) .
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Suppose that \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(U\) is a subspace of \(V{}\text{.}\) Show that \(P_{U^\perp} = I - P_{U}\text{,}\) where \(I\) is the identity operator on \(V{}\text{.}\)

    6.

    7.

    Suppose that \(X\) and \(Y\) are finite-dimensional subspaces of \(V{}\text{.}\) Prove that \(P_{\!X} P_{\!Y} = 0\) if and only if \(\langle x, y \rangle = 0\) for all \(x \in X\) and all \(y \in Y\text{.}\)
    🔗

    8.

    Suppose \(U\) is a finite-dimensional subspace of \(V\) and \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Define a linear functional \(\fun \phi U \F{}\) by
    \begin{equation*} \phi(u) = \langle u, v \rangle \end{equation*}
    for all \(u \in U\text{.}\) By the Riesz representation theorem Theorem 6.42 as applied to the inner product space \(U\text{,}\) there exists a unique vector \(w \in U\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \phi(u) = \langle u, w \rangle \end{equation*}
    for all \(u \in U\text{.}\) Show that \(w = P_{U} v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Suppose \(V\) is finite-dimensional. Suppose \(P \in \Lc(V)\) is such that \(P^2 = P\) and every vector in \(\ker P\) is orthogonal to every vector in \(\ran P{}\text{.}\) Prove that there exists a subspace \(U\) of \(V\) such that \(P = P_{U}\text{.}\)

    10.

    11.

    12.

    13.

    Suppose \(\F{} = \R{}\) and \(V\) is finite-dimensional. For each \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) let \(\phi_v\) denote the linear functional on \(V\) defined by
    \begin{equation*} \phi_v(u) = \langle u, v \rangle \end{equation*}
    for all \(u \in V{}\text{.}\)
    1. Show that \(v \mapsto \phi_v\) is an injective linear map from \(V\) to \(V'\!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Use (a) and a dimension-counting argument to show that \(v \mapsto \phi_v\) is an isomorphism from \(V\) onto \(V'\!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Note: The purpose of this exercise is to give an alternative proof of the Riesz representation theorem ( Theorem 6.42 and Theorem 6.58) when \(\F{} = \R{}\text{.}\) Thus you should not use the Riesz representation theorem as a tool in your solution.
    🔗
