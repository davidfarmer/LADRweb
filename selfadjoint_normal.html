    Section 7.1 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators

    Subsection 7.1.1 Adjoints

    Definition 7.1. adjoint, \(T^\ast\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) The adjoint of \(T\) is the function \(T^* \colon W \to V\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \langle Tv, w \rangle = \langle v, T^*w \rangle \end{equation*}
    for every \(v \in V\) and every \(w \in W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The word adjoint has another meaning in linear algebra. In case you encounter the second meaning elsewhere, be warned that the two meanings for adjoint are unrelated to each other.
    🔗
    To see why the definition above makes sense, suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Fix \(w \in W{}\text{.}\) Consider the linear functional
    \begin{equation*} v \mapsto \langle Tv,w \rangle \end{equation*}
    on \(V\) that maps \(v \in V\) to \(\langle Tv, w \rangle\text{;}\) this linear functional depends on \(T\) and \(w\text{.}\) By the Riesz representation theorem Theorem 6.42, there exists a unique vector in \(V\) such that this linear functional is given by taking the inner product with it. We call this unique vector \(T^*w\text{.}\) In other words, \(T^*w\) is the unique vector in \(V\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \langle Tv, w \rangle = \langle v, T^*w \rangle \end{equation*}
    for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In the equation above, the inner product on the left takes place in \(W\) and the inner product on the right takes place in \(V\text{.}\) However, we use the same notation \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\) for both inner products.
    🔗

    Example 7.2. adjoint of a linear map from \(\R3\) to \(\R2\).

    Define \(T \colon \R{3} \rightarrow \R{2}\) by
    \begin{equation*} T(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (x_2 + 3x_3, 2x_1). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To compute \(T^*{}\text{,}\) suppose \((x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \R3\) and {\((y_1, y_2) \in \RR{2}\)}. Then
    \begin{align*} \langle T(x_1, x_2, x_3), (y_1, y_2) \rangle \amp = \langle (x_2 + 3x_3, 2x_1), (y_1, y_2) \rangle\\ \amp = x_2 y_1 + 3x_3 y_1 + 2 x_1 y_2\\ \amp = \langle (x_1, x_2, x_3), (2y_2, y_1, 3y_1) \rangle \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The equation above and the definition of the adjoint imply that
    \begin{equation*} T^*(y_1, y_2) = (2y_2, y_1, 3y_1). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.3. adjoint of a linear map with range of dimension at most \(1\).

    Fix \(u \in V\) and \(x \in W{}\text{.}\) Define \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\) by
    \begin{equation*} Tv = \langle v, u \rangle x \end{equation*}
    for each \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) To compute \(T^*{}\text{,}\) suppose \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \langle Tv, w \rangle \amp = \bigl\langle \langle v, u \rangle x, w \bigr \rangle\\ \amp = \langle v, u \rangle \langle x, w \rangle\\ \amp = \bigl\langle v, \langle w, x \rangle u \bigr\rangle\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus
    \begin{equation*} T^*w = \langle w, x \rangle u. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The two examples above and the proof below use a common technique for computing \(T^*\text{:}\) start with a formula for \(\langle Tv,w \rangle\) then manipulate it to get just \(v\) in the first slot; the entry in the second slot will then be \(T^*w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In the two examples above, \(T^*\) turned out to be not just a function from \(W\) to \(V\) but a linear map from \(W\) to \(V{}\text{.}\) This behavior is true in general, as shown by the next result.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.4. adjoint of a linear map is a linear map.

    If \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{,}\) then \(T^* \in \Lc(W{},V)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{.}\) If \(v \in V\) and \(w_1, w_2 \in W{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{align*} \langle Tv, w_1 + w_2 \rangle \amp = \langle Tv, w_1 \rangle + \langle Tv, w_2 \rangle\\ \amp = \langle v, T^*w_1 \rangle + \langle v, T^*w_2 \rangle\\ \amp = \langle v, T^*w_1 + T^*w_2 \rangle \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The equation above shows that
    \begin{equation*} T^*(w_1 + w_2) = T^*w_1 + T^*w_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) and \(w \in W\text{,}\) then
    \begin{align*} \langle Tv, \lambda w \rangle \amp = \bar{\lambda} \langle Tv, w \rangle\\ \amp = \bar{\lambda} \langle v, T^*w \rangle\\ \amp = \langle v, \lambda T^*w \rangle \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The equation above shows that
    \begin{equation*} T^*(\lambda w) = \lambda T^*w. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(T^*\) is a linear map, as desired.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 7.5. properties of the adjoint.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then
    1. \((S + T)^* = S^* + T^*\) for all \(S\in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \((\lambda T)^* = \bar{\lambda} T^*\) for all \(\lambda \in \F{}\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(( T^* )^* = T\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \((ST)^* = T^* S^*\) for all \(S \in \Lc(W{}, U)\) (here \(U\) is a finite-dimensional inner product space over \(\F{}\));
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(I^* = I\text{,}\) where \(I\) is the identity operator on \(V\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. if \(T\) is invertible, then \(T^*\) is invertible and \(( T^* )^{-1} = ( T^{-1} )^*{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\text{.}\)
    1. If \(S \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{,}\) then
      \begin{align*} \langle (S+T)v, w \rangle \amp = \langle Sv, w \rangle + \langle Tv, w \rangle\\ \amp = \langle v, S^*w \rangle + \langle v,T^*w \rangle\\ \amp = \langle v, S^*w + T^*w \rangle \text{.} \end{align*}
      Thus \((S+T)^*w = S^*w + T^*w\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} \langle (\lambda T) v, w \rangle = \lambda \langle Tv, w \rangle = \lambda \langle v, T^* w \rangle = \langle v, \bar{\lambda} T^*w \rangle . \end{equation*}
      Thus \((\lambda T)^* w = \bar{\lambda} T^*w\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We have
      \begin{equation*} \langle T^*w,v \rangle = \overline{ \langle v,T^*w \rangle } = \overline{ \langle Tv,w \rangle } = \langle w, Tv \rangle . \end{equation*}
      Thus \(( T^* )^*v = Tv\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Suppose \(S \in \Lc(W{},U)\) and \(u \in U\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \langle (ST) v, u \rangle = \langle S(Tv), u \rangle = \langle Tv, S^*u \rangle = \langle v, T^*( S^*u ) \rangle . \end{equation*}
      Thus \((ST)^* u = T^*( S^*u )\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Suppose \(u \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \langle Iu, v \rangle = \langle u, v \rangle . \end{equation*}
      Thus \(I^*v = v\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. Suppose \(T\) is invertible. Take adjoints of both sides of the equation \(T^{-1}T = I\text{,}\) then use (d) and (e) to show that \(T^* ( T^{-1} )^* = I\text{.}\) Similarly, the equation \(T T^{-1} = I\) implies \(( T^{-1} )^* T^* = I\text{.}\) Thus \(( T^{-1} )^*\) is the inverse of \(T^*{}\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(\F{} = \R{}\text{,}\) then the map \(T \mapsto T^*\) is a linear map from \(\Lc(V, W)\) to \(\Lc(W,V)\text{,}\) as follows from (a) and (b) of the result above. However, if \(\F{} = \C{}\text{,}\) then this map is not linear because of the complex conjugate that appears in (b).
    🔗
    The next result shows the relationship between the null space and the range of a linear map and its adjoint.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.6. null space and range of \(T^*\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then
    1. \(\ker T^* = (\ran T)^\perp\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\ran T^* = (\ker T)^\perp\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\ker T = ( \ran T^* )^\perp\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\ran T = ( \ker T^* )^\perp\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We begin by proving (a). Let \(w \in W{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} w \in \ker T^* \amp \iff T^*w = 0\\ \amp \iff \langle v, T^*w \rangle = 0 \textit{ for all\ } v \in V\\ \amp \iff \langle Tv, w \rangle = 0 \textit{ for all\ } v \in V\\ \amp \iff w \in (\ran T)^\perp\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus \(\ker T^* = (\ran T)^\perp\text{,}\) proving (a).
    🔗
    If we take the orthogonal complement of both sides of (a), we get (d), where we have used 6.52. Replacing \(T\) with \(T^*\) in (a) gives (c), where we have used 7.5(c). Finally, replacing \(T\) with \(T^*\) in (d) gives (b).
    🔗
    🔗
    As we will soon see, the next definition is intimately connected to the matrix of the adjoint of a linear map.
    🔗

    Definition 7.7. conjugate transpose, \(A^*\).

    The conjugate transpose of an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix \(A\) is the \(n\)-by-\(m\) matrix \(A^*\) obtained by interchanging the rows and columns and then taking the complex conjugate of each entry. In other words, if \(j \in \{ 1, \ldots, n \}\) and \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} (A^*)_{j,k} = \overline{A_{k,j}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 7.8. conjugate transpose of a 2-by-3 matrix.

    Aside
    If a matrix \(A\) has only real entries, then \(A^* = \tran{A}{}\text{,}\) where \(\tran{A}\) denotes the transpose of \(A\) (the matrix obtained by interchanging the rows and the columns) .
    🔗
    🔗
    The conjugate transpose of the \(2\)-by-\(3\) matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}2 \amp 3 + 4i \amp 7 \\ 6 \amp 5 \amp 8i \end{pmatrix}\) is the \(3\)-by-\(2\) matrix
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp 6 \\ 3 - 4i \amp 5 \\ 7 \amp -8i \end{pmatrix} .\\[0.05in] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The adjoint of a linear map does not depend on a choice of basis. Thus we frequently emphasize adjoints of linear maps instead of transposes or conjugate transposes of matrices.
    🔗
    The next result shows how to compute the matrix of \(T^*\) from the matrix of \(T{}\text{.}\) Caution: With respect to nonorthonormal bases, the matrix of \(T^*\) does not necessarily equal the conjugate transpose of the matrix of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 7.9. matrix of \(T^*\) equals conjugate transpose of matrix of \(T\).

    Let \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Suppose \(e_1, \dots, e_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V\) and \(f_1, \dots, f_m\) is an orthonormal basis of  \(W{}\text{.}\) Then \(\M\bigl(T^*{}, (f_1, \dots, f_m), (e_1, \dots, e_n)\bigr)\) is the conjugate transpose of \(\M\bigl(T, (e_1, \dots, e_n), (f_1, \dots, f_m)\bigr)\text{.}\) In other words,
    \begin{equation*} \M( T^* ) = ( \M(T) )^*{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    In this proof, we will write \(\M(T)\) and \(\M( T^* )\) instead of the longer expressions \(\M\bigl(T, (e_1, \dots, e_n), (f_1, \dots, f_m)\bigr)\) and \(\M\bigl(T^*{}, (f_1, \dots, f_m), (e_1, \dots, e_n)\bigr)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Recall that we obtain the \(k^\nth\) column of \(\M(T)\) by writing \(Te_k\) as a linear combination of the \(f_j\)’s; the scalars used in this linear combination then become the \(k^\nth\) column of \(\M(T)\text{.}\) Because \(f_1, \dots, f_m\) is an orthonormal basis of \(W{}\text{,}\) we know how to write \(Te_k\) as a linear combination of the \(f_j\)’s [see 6.30(a)] :
    \begin{equation*} Te_k = \langle Te_k, f_1 \rangle f_1 + \dots + \langle Te_k, f_m \rangle f_m. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    the entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) of \(M(T)\) is \(\langle Te_k, f_j \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In the statement above, replace \(T\) with \(T^*\) and interchange \(e_1, \dots, e_n\) and \(f_1, \dots, f_m\text{.}\) This shows that the entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) of \(\M( T^* )\) is \(\langle T^* \! f_k, e_j \rangle\text{,}\) which equals \(\langle f_k, Te_j \rangle\text{,}\) which equals \(\overline{\langle Te_j, f_k \rangle }\text{,}\) which equals the complex conjugate of the entry in row \(k\text{,}\) column \(j\text{,}\) of \(\M(T)\text{.}\) Thus \(\M( T^* ) = ( \M(T) )^*{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The Riesz representation theorem as stated in Theorem 6.58 provides an identification of \(V\) with its dual space \(V'\) defined in Definition 3.110. Under this identification, the orthogonal complement \(U^\perp\) of a subset \(U \subset V\) corresponds to the annihilator \(U^0\) of \(U\text{.}\) If \(U\) is a subspace of \(V{}\text{,}\) then the formulas for the dimensions of \(U^\perp\) and \(U^0\) become identical under this identification—see 3.125 and Theorem 6.51.
    🔗

    Aside
    Because orthogonal complements and adjoints are easier to deal with than annihilators and dual maps, there is no need to work with annihilators and dual maps in the context of inner product spaces.
    🔗
    Suppose \(\fun T V W\) is a linear map. Under the identification of \(V\) with \(V'\) and the identification of \(W\) with \(W'\!\text{,}\) the adjoint map \(\fun {T^*} W V\) corresponds to the dual map \(\fun {T'} {W'} {V'}\) defined in Definition 3.118, as Exercise 7.1.3.32 asks you to verify. Under this identification, the formulas for \(\ker T^*\) and \(\ran T^*\) [ Theorem 7.6(a) and (b)] then become identical to the formulas for \(\ker T'\) and \(\ran T'\) [ Theorem 3.128(a) and Theorem 3.130(b)] . Furthermore, the theorem about the matrix of \(T^*\) Theorem 7.9 is analogous to the theorem about the matrix of \(T'\) Theorem 3.132.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.2 Self-Adjoint Operators

    Now we switch our attention to operators on inner product spaces. Instead of considering linear maps from \(V\) to \(W{}\text{,}\) we will focus on linear maps from \(V\) to \(V\text{;}\) recall that such linear maps are called operators.
    🔗

    Definition 7.10. self-adjoint.

    An operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is called self-adjoint if \(T = T^*{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(e_1, \ldots, e_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V{}\text{,}\) then \(T\) is self-adjoint if and only if \(\M( T, (e_1, \ldots, e_n) ) = \M( T, (e_1, \ldots, e_n) )^*{}\text{,}\) as follows from Theorem 7.9.
    🔗

    Example 7.11. determining whether \(T\) is self-adjoint from its matrix.

    Suppose \(c \in \F{}\) and \(T\) is the operator on \(\F{2}\) whose matrix (with respect to the standard basis) is
    \begin{equation*} \M(T) = \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp c\\ 3 \amp 7 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The matrix of \(T^*\) (with respect to the standard basis) is
    \begin{equation*} \M( T^* ) = \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp 3\\ \overline{c} \amp 7 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(\M(T) = \M( T^* )\) if and only if \(c = 3\text{.}\) Hence the operator \(T\) is self-adjoint if and only if \(c = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    A good analogy to keep in mind is that the adjoint on \(\Lc(V)\) plays a role similar to that of the complex conjugate on \(\C{}\text{.}\) A complex number \(z\) is real if and only if \(z = \bar{z}\text{;}\) thus a self-adjoint operator (\(T = T^*\)) is analogous to a real number.
    🔗

    Aside
    An operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is self-adjoint if and only if
    \begin{equation*} \langle Tv, w \rangle = \langle v, Tw \rangle \end{equation*}
    for all \(v, w \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We will see that the analogy discussed above is reflected in some important properties of self-adjoint operators, beginning with eigenvalues in the next result.
    🔗
    If \(\F{} = \R{}\text{,}\) then by definition every eigenvalue is real, so the next result is interesting only when \(\F{} = \C{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 7.12. eigenvalues of self-adjoint operators.

    Every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(T\) is a self-adjoint operator on \(V{}\text{.}\) Let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue of \(T\text{,}\) and let \(v\) be a nonzero vector in \(V\) such that \(Tv = \lambda v\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \lambda \| v \|^2 = \langle \lambda v, v \rangle = \langle Tv, v \rangle = \langle v, Tv \rangle = \langle v, \lambda v \rangle = \bar{\lambda} \| v \|^2{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(\lambda = \bar{\lambda}\text{,}\) which means that \(\lambda\) is real, as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result is false for real inner product spaces. As an example, consider the operator \(T \in \Lc( \R{2} )\) that is a counterclockwise rotation of \(90^\circ\) around the origin; thus \(T(x, y) = (-y, x)\text{.}\) Notice that \(Tv\) is orthogonal to \(v\) for every \(v \in \RR{2}\text{,}\) even though \(T \ne 0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 7.13. \(Tv\) is orthogonal to \(v\) for all \(v \iff T =0\) (assuming \(\F{} = \C{}\)).

    Suppose \(V\) is a complex inner product space and \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \langle Tv, v \rangle = 0 \text{ for every } v \in V \iff T = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    If \(u, w \in V{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{align*} \langle Tu, w \rangle =\ \amp \frac{\langle T(u+w), u + w \rangle - \langle T(u-w), u - w \rangle }{4}\\ \amp + \frac{\langle T(u+iw), u+iw \rangle - \langle T(u-iw), u - iw \rangle }{4} i\text{,} \end{align*}
    as can be verified by computing the right side. Note that each term on the right side is of the form \(\langle Tv, v \rangle\) for appropriate \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now suppose \(\langle Tv, v \rangle = 0\) for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then the equation above implies that \(\langle Tu, w \rangle = 0\) for all \(u, w \in V{}\text{,}\) which then implies that \(Tu = 0\) for every \(u \in V\) (take \(w = Tu\)). Hence \(T = 0\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The next result provides another good example of how self-adjoint operators behave like real numbers.
    🔗
    The next result is false for real inner product spaces, as shown by considering any operator on a real inner product space that is not self-adjoint.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.14. \(\langle Tv, v \rangle\) is real for all \(v \iff T\) is self-adjoint (assuming \(\F{} = \C{}\)).

    Suppose \(V\) is a complex inner product space and \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} T \text{ is self-adjoint } \iff \langle Tv, v \rangle \in \R{} \text{ for every } v \in V{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    If \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation} \langle T^*v, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v, T^*v \rangle } = \overline{\langle Tv, v \rangle }.\tag{7.15} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Now
    \begin{align*} T \text{ is self-adjoint } \amp \iff T - T^* = 0\\ \amp \iff \langle (T - T^*)v, v \rangle = 0 \text{ for every } v \in V\\ \amp \iff \langle Tv, v \rangle - \overline{\langle Tv, v \rangle } = 0 \text{ for every } v \in V\\ \amp \iff \langle Tv, v \rangle \in \R{} \text{ for every } v \in V{}\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the second equivalence follows from Theorem 7.13 as applied to \(T - T^*\) and the third equivalence follows from (7.15).
    🔗
    🔗
    On a real inner product space \(V{}\text{,}\) a nonzero operator \(T\) might satisfy \(\langle Tv, v \rangle = 0\) for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) However, the next result shows that this cannot happen for a self-adjoint operator.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.16. \(T\) self-adjoint and \(\langle Tv, v \rangle = 0\) for all \(v \iff T = 0\).

    Suppose \(T\) is a self-adjoint operator on \(V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \langle Tv, v \rangle = 0 \text{ for every } v \in V \iff T = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We have already proved this (without the hypothesis that \(T\) is self-adjoint) when \(V\) is a complex inner product space (see 7.13). Thus we can assume that \(V\) is a real inner product space. If \(u, w \in V{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation} \langle Tu, w \rangle = \frac{ \langle T(u+w), u+w \rangle -\langle T(u-w), u-w \rangle }{4},\tag{7.17} \end{equation}
    as can be proved by computing the right side using the equation
    \begin{equation*} \langle Tw, u \rangle = \langle w, Tu \rangle = \langle Tu, w \rangle , \end{equation*}
    where the first equality holds because \(T\) is self-adjoint and the second equality holds because we are working in a real inner product space.
    🔗
    Now suppose \(\langle Tv, v \rangle = 0\) for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Because each term on the right side of (7.17) is of the form \(\langle Tv, v \rangle\) for appropriate \(v\text{,}\) this implies that \(\langle Tu, w \rangle = 0\) for all \(u, w \in V{}\text{.}\) This implies that \(Tu = 0\) for every \(u \in V\) (take \(w = Tu\)). Hence \(T = 0\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗

    Subsection 7.1.3 Normal Operators

    Definition 7.18. normal.

    1. An operator on an inner product space is called normal if it commutes with its adjoint.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. In other words, \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is normal if \(TT^* = T^*T{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Every self-adjoint operator is normal, because if \(T\) is self-adjoint then \(T^* = T{}\) and hence \(T\) commutes with \(T^*{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 7.19. an operator that is normal but not self-adjoint.

    {Let \(T\) be the operator on \(\F{2}\) whose matrix (with respect to the standard basis) is
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp -3 \\ 3 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(T(w, z) = (2w-3z, 3w + 2z)\text{.}\) }
    🔗
    This operator \(T\) is not self-adjoint because the entry in row 2, column 1 (which equals \(3\)) does not equal the complex conjugate of the entry in row 1, column 2 (which equals \(-3\)).
    🔗
    The matrix of \(TT^*\) equals
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp -3 \\ 3 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} , \textit{ which equals\ } \begin{pmatrix}13 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Similarly, the matrix of \(T^*T\) equals
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}2 \amp -3 \\ 3 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix} , \textit{ which equals\ } \begin{pmatrix}13 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 13 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because \(TT^*\) and \(T^*T\) have the same matrix, we see that \(TT^* = T^*T{}\text{.}\) Thus \(T\) is normal.
    🔗
    🔗
    In the next section we will see why normal operators are worthy of special attention. The next result provides a useful characterization of normal operators.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.20. \(T\) is normal if and only if \(Tv\) and \(T^*v\) have the same norm.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} T \text{ is normal } \iff \|Tv\| = \|T^*v\| \text{ for every } v \in V{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We have
    \begin{align*} T \textit{ is normal} \amp \iff T^*T - TT^* = 0\\ \amp \iff \langle (T^*T - TT^*)v, v \rangle = 0 \text{ for every } v \in V\\ \amp \iff \langle T^*T v, v \rangle = \langle TT^* v, v \rangle \text{ for every } v \in V\\ \amp \iff \langle T v, Tv \rangle = \langle T^* v, T^*v \rangle \text{ for every } v \in V\\ \amp \iff \|Tv\|^2 = \lVert T^*v \rVert ^2 \text{ for every } v\in V\\ \amp \iff \|Tv\| = \lVert T^*v \rVert \text{ for every } v\in V{}\text{,} \end{align*}
    where we used 7.16 to establish the second equivalence (note that the operator \(T^*T - TT^*\) is self-adjoint).
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result presents several consequences of the result above. Compare (e) of the next result to Exercise 7.1.3.3. That exercise states that the eigenvalues of the adjoint of each operator are equal (as a set) to the complex conjugates of the eigenvalues of the operator. The exercise says nothing about eigenvectors, because an operator and its adjoint may have different eigenvectors. However, (e) of the next result implies that a normal operator and its adjoint have the same eigenvectors.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.21. range, null space, and eigenvectors of a normal operator.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is normal. Then
    1. \(\ker T = \ker T^*\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\ran T = \ran T^*\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(V = \ker T \oplus \ran T\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(T - \lambda I\) is normal for every \(\lambda \in \F{}\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. if \(v \in V\) and \(\lambda \in \F{}\text{,}\) then \(Tv = \lambda v\) if and only if \(T^*v = \bar{\lambda} v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Suppose \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} v \in \ker T \iff \lVert Tv \rVert = 0 \iff \lVert T^*v \rVert = 0 \iff v \in \ker T^*{}, \end{equation*}
      where the middle equivalence above follows from Theorem 7.20. Thus \(\ker T = \ker T^*{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We have
      \begin{equation*} \ran T = ( \ker T^* )^\perp = (\ker T)^\perp = \ran T^*{}, \end{equation*}
      where the first equality comes from Theorem 7.6(d), the second equality comes from (a) in this result, and the third equality comes from Theorem 7.6(b).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We have
      \begin{equation*} V = (\ker T) \oplus (\ker T)^\perp = \ker T \oplus \ran T^* = \ker T \oplus \ran T, \end{equation*}
      where the first equality comes from Theorem 6.49, the second equality comes from Theorem 7.6(b), and the third equality comes from (b) in this result.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Suppose \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{align*} (T - \lambda I)(T-\lambda I)^* \amp = (T - \lambda I)( T^* - \bar{\lambda}I )\\ \amp = TT^* - \bar{\lambda}T - \lambda T^* + \lvert \lambda \rvert ^2 I\\ \amp = T^*T - \bar{\lambda}T - \lambda T^* + \lvert \lambda \rvert ^2 I\\ \amp = ( T^* - \bar{\lambda}I )(T - \lambda I)\\ \amp = (T - \lambda I)^* (T-\lambda I)\text{.} \end{align*}
      Thus \(T - \lambda I\) commutes with its adjoint. Hence \(T - \lambda I\) is normal.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Suppose \(v \in V\) and \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then (d) and Theorem 7.20 imply that
      \begin{equation*} \| (T - \lambda I) v \| = \lVert (T - \lambda I)^* v \rVert = \lVert ( T^* - \bar{\lambda}I ) v \rVert . \end{equation*}
      Thus \(\| (T - \lambda I) v \| = 0\) if and only if \(\lVert ( T^* - \bar{\lambda}I ) v \rVert = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(Tv = \lambda v\) if and only if \(T^*v = \bar{\lambda} v\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Because every self-adjoint operator is normal, the next result applies in particular to self-adjoint operators.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.22. orthogonal eigenvectors for normal operators.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is normal. Then eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(\alpha , \beta\) are distinct eigenvalues of \(T\text{,}\) with corresponding eigenvectors \(u, v\text{.}\) Thus \(Tu = \alpha u\) and \(Tv = \beta v\text{.}\) From 7.21(e) we have \(T^*v = \bar{\beta}v\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{align*} (\alpha - \beta)\langle u,v \rangle \amp = \langle \alpha u, v \rangle - \langle u, \bar{\beta} v \rangle\\ \amp = \langle Tu, v \rangle - \langle u, T^*v \rangle\\ \amp = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Because \(\alpha \ne \beta\text{,}\) the equation above implies that \(\langle u,v \rangle = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(u\) and \(v\) are orthogonal, as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    As stated here, the next result makes sense only when \(\F{} = \C{}\text{.}\) However, see Exercise 7.1.3.12 for a version that makes sense when \(\F{} = \C{}\) and when \(\F{} = \R{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose \(\F{} = \C{}\) and \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Under the analogy between \(\Lc(V)\) and \(\C{}\text{,}\) with the adjoint on \(\Lc(V)\) playing a similar role to that of the complex conjugate on \(\C{}\text{,}\) the operators \(A\) and \(B\) as defined by (7.24) correspond to the real and imaginary parts of \(T{}\text{.}\) Thus the informal title of the result below should make sense.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.23. \(T\) is normal \(\iff\) the real and imaginary parts of \(T\) commute.

    Suppose \(\F{} = \C{}\) and \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then \(T\) is normal if and only if there exist commuting self-adjoint operators \(A\) and \(B\) such that \(T = A + iB\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First suppose \(T\) is normal. Let
    \begin{equation} A = \frac{T + T^*}{2} \andd B = \frac{T - T^*}{2i}.\tag{7.24} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Then \(A\) and \(B\) are self-adjoint and \(T = A + iB\text{.}\) A quick computation shows that
    \begin{equation} AB - BA = \frac{T^*T - TT^*}{2i}.\tag{7.25} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Because \(T\) is normal, the right side of the equation above equals \(0\text{.}\) Thus the operators \(A\) and \(B\) commute, as desired.
    🔗
    To prove the implication in the other direction, now suppose there exist commuting self-adjoint operators \(A\) and \(B\) such that \(T = A + iB\text{.}\) Then \(T^* = A - iB\text{.}\) Adding the last two equations and then dividing by \(2\) produces the equation for \(A\) in (7.24). Subtracting the last two equations and then dividing by \(2i\) produces the equation for \(B\) in (7.24). Now (7.24) implies (7.25). Because \(B\) and \(A\) commute, (7.25) implies that \(T\) is normal, as desired.

    \begin{equation*} \|v\| = \|w \| = 2, Tv = 3v, Tw = 4w. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Show that \(\|T(v + w)\| = 10\text{.}\)

    24.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and
    \begin{equation*} a_0 + a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \dots + a_{m-1} z^{m-1} + z^m \end{equation*}
    is the minimal polynomial of \(T{}\text{.}\) Prove that the minimal polynomial of \(T^*\) is
    \begin{equation*} \overline{a_0} + \overline{a_1} z + \overline{a_2} z^2 + \dots + \overline{a_{m-1}} z^{m-1} + z^m\!. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note: This exercise shows that the minimal polynomial of \(T^*\) equals the minimal polynomial of \(T\) if \(\F{} = \R{}\text{.}\)

    25.

    31.
