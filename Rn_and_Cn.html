    Section 1.1 \(\R n\) and \(\C n\)

    Subsection 1.1.1 Complex Numbers

    You should already be familiar with basic properties of the set \(\R{}\) of real numbers. Complex numbers were invented so that we can take square roots of negative numbers. The idea is to assume we have a square root of \(-1\text{,}\) denoted by \(i\text{,}\) that obeys the usual rules of arithmetic. Here are the formal definitions.
    🔗

    Definition 1.1. complex numbers, \(\symbf{C}\).

    1. A complex number is an ordered pair \((a, b)\text{,}\) where \(a, b \in \R{}\text{,}\) but we will write this as \(a + bi\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The set of all complex numbers is denoted by \(\C{}\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \C{} = \{ a + bi: a, b \in \R{} \}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Addition and multiplication on \(\C{}\) are defined by
      \begin{gather*} (a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i,\\ (a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i; \end{gather*}
      here \(a, b, c, d \in \R{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(a \in \R{}\text{,}\) we identify \(a + 0i\) with the real number \(a\text{.}\) Thus we think of \(\R{}\) as a subset of \(\C{}\text{.}\) We usually write \(0 + bi\) as just \(bi\text{,}\) and we usually write \(0 + 1i\) as just \(i\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Aside
    The symbol \(i\) was first used to denote \(\sqrt{-1}\) by Leonhard Euler in 1777.
    🔗
    To motivate the definition of complex multiplication given above, pretend that we knew that \(i^2 = -1\) and then use the usual rules of arithmetic to derive the formula above for the product of two complex numbers. Then use that formula to verify that we indeed have
    \begin{equation*} i^2 = -1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Do not memorize the formula for the product of two complex numbers—you can always rederive it by recalling that \(i^2 = -1\) and then using the usual rules of arithmetic (as given by Theorem 1.3). The next example illustrates this procedure.
    🔗

    Example 1.2. complex arithmetic.

    The product \((2 + 3i)(4+5i)\) can be evaluated by applying the distributive and commutative properties from Theorem 1.3:
    \begin{align*} (2 + 3i)(4+5i) \amp = 2 \cdot (4+5i) +(3i)(4+5i)\\ \amp = 2 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 5i + 3i\cdot 4 + (3i)(5i)\\ \amp = 8 + 10i + 12i -15\\ \amp = -7 + 22i\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Our first result states that complex addition and complex multiplication have the familiar properties that we expect.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3. properties of complex arithmetic.

    1. commutativity.
      \(\alpha + \beta = \beta + \alpha\) and \(\alpha \beta = \beta \alpha\) for all \(\alpha , \beta \in \C{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. associativity.
      \((\alpha + \beta) + \lambda = \alpha + (\beta + \lambda)\) and \((\alpha \beta)\lambda = \alpha (\beta \lambda)\) for all \(\alpha , \beta, \lambda \in \C{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. identities.
      \(\lambda + 0 = \lambda\) and \(\lambda 1 = \lambda\) for all \(\lambda \in \C{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. additive inverse.
      For every \(\alpha \in \C{}\text{,}\) there exists a unique \(\beta \in \C{}\) such that \(\alpha + \beta = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. multiplicative inverse.
      For every \(\alpha \in \C{}\) with \(\alpha \ne 0\text{,}\) there exists a unique \(\beta \in \C{}\) such that \(\alpha \beta = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. distributive property.
      \(\lambda(\alpha + \beta) = \lambda \alpha + \lambda \beta\) for all \(\lambda, \alpha , \beta \in \C{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The properties above are proved using the familiar properties of real numbers and the definitions of complex addition and multiplication. The next example shows how commutativity of complex multiplication is proved. Proofs of the other properties above are left as exercises.
    🔗

    Example 1.4. commutativity of complex multiplication.

    To show that \(\alpha \beta = \beta \alpha\) for all \(\alpha , \beta \in \C{}\text{,}\) suppose
    \begin{equation*} \alpha = a + bi \andd \beta = c + di, \end{equation*}
    where \(a, b, c, d \in \R{}\text{.}\) Then the definition of multiplication of complex numbers shows that
    \begin{align*} \alpha \beta \amp = (a + bi)(c + di)\\ \amp = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i \end{align*}
    and
    \begin{align*} \beta\alpha \amp = (c + di)(a + bi)\\ \amp = (ca - db) + (cb + da)i\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The equations above and the commutativity of multiplication and addition of real numbers show that \(\alpha \beta = \beta \alpha\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Next, we define the additive and multiplicative inverses of complex numbers, and then use those inverses to define subtraction and division operations with complex numbers.
    🔗

    Definition 1.5. \(-\alpha \text{,}\) subtraction, \(1/\alpha \text{,}\) division.

    Suppose \(\alpha , \beta \in \C{}\text{.}\)
    1. Let \(-\alpha\) denote the additive inverse of \(\alpha\text{.}\) Thus \(-\alpha\) is the unique complex number such that
      \begin{equation*} \alpha + (-\alpha ) = 0. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Subtraction on \(\C{}\) is defined by
      \begin{equation*} \beta - \alpha = \beta + (-\alpha ). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For \(\alpha \ne 0\text{,}\) let \(1/\alpha\) and \(\frac{1}{\alpha }\) denote the multiplicative inverse of \(\alpha\text{.}\) Thus \(1/\alpha\) is the unique complex number such that
      \begin{equation*} \alpha (1/\alpha ) = 1. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. For \(\alpha \ne 0\text{,}\) division by \(\alpha\) is defined by
      \begin{equation*} \beta / \alpha = \beta (1/\alpha ). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    So that we can conveniently make definitions and prove theorems that apply to both real and complex numbers, we adopt the following notation.
    🔗

    Notation 1.6.

    Throughout this book, \(\F{}\) stands for either \(\R{}\) or \(\C{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The letter \(\F{}\) is used because \(\R{}\) and \(\C{}\) are examples of what are called fields.
    🔗
    Thus if we prove a theorem involving \(\F{}{}\text{,}\) we will know that it holds when \(\F{}\) is replaced with \(\R{}\) and when \(\F{}\) is replaced with \(\C{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Elements of \(\F{}\) are called scalars. The word “scalar” (which is just a fancy word for “number”) is often used when we want to emphasize that an object is a number, as opposed to a vector (vectors will be defined soon).
    🔗
    For \(\alpha \in \F{}\) and \(m\) a positive integer, we define \(\alpha ^m\) to denote the product of \(\alpha\) with itself \(m\) times:
    \begin{equation*} \alpha ^m = \underbrace{\alpha \cdots \alpha }_{m \text{ times } }\!\!. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This definition implies that
    \begin{equation*} ( \alpha ^m )^n = \alpha ^{mn} \andd (\alpha \beta)^m = \alpha ^m \beta^m \end{equation*}
    for all \(\alpha , \beta \in \F{}\) and all positive integers \(m, n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.2 Lists

    Before defining \(\R{n}\) and \(\CC{n}\text{,}\) we look at two important examples.
    🔗

    Example 1.7. \(\R{2}\) and \(\R{3}\).

    1. The set \(\RR{2}\text{,}\) which you can think of as a plane, is the set of all ordered pairs of real numbers:
      \begin{equation*} \R{2} = \{ (x,y): x, y \in \R{} \}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The set \(\RR{3}\text{,}\) which you can think of as ordinary space, is the set of all ordered triples of real numbers:
      \begin{equation*} \R{3} = \{ (x,y, z): x, y, z \in \R{} \}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    To generalize \(\R{2}\) and \(\R{3}\) to higher dimensions, we first need to discuss the concept of lists.
    🔗

    Definition 1.8. list, length.

    1. Suppose \(n\) is a nonnegative integer. A list of length \(n\) is an ordered collection of \(n\) elements (which might be numbers, other lists, or more abstract objects).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Two lists are equal if and only if they have the same length and the same elements in the same order.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Many mathematicians call a list of length \(n\) an \(n\)-tuple.
    🔗
    🔗
    Lists are often written as elements separated by commas and surrounded by parentheses. Thus a list of length two is an ordered pair that might be written as \((a, b)\text{.}\) A list of length three is an ordered triple that might be written as \((x,y,z)\text{.}\) A list of length \(n\) might look like this:
    \begin{equation*} (z_1, \dots, z_n). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Sometimes we will use the word list without specifying its length. Remember, however, that by definition each list has a finite length that is a nonnegative integer. Thus an object that looks like \((x_1, x_2, \dotsc )\text{,}\) which might be said to have infinite length, is not a list.
    🔗
    A list of length \(0\) looks like this: \((\ )\text{.}\) We consider such an object to be a list so that some of our theorems will not have trivial exceptions.
    🔗
    Lists differ from finite sets in two ways: in lists, order matters and repetitions have meaning; in sets, order and repetitions are irrelevant.
    🔗

    Example 1.9. lists versus sets.

    1. The lists \((3,5)\) and \((5,3)\) are not equal, but the sets \(\{3,5\}\) and \(\{5,3\}\) are equal.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The lists \((4,4)\) and \((4, 4, 4)\) are not equal (they do not have the same length), although the sets \(\{4,4\}\) and \(\{4, 4, 4\}\) both equal the set \(\{4\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.3 \(\F n\)

    To define the higher-dimensional analogues of \(\R{2}\) and \(\RR{3}\text{,}\) we will simply replace \(\R{}\) with \(\F{}\) (which equals \(\R{}\) or \(\C{}\)) and replace the \(2\) or \(3\) with an arbitrary positive integer.
    🔗

    Notation 1.10. \(n\).

    Fix a positive integer \(n\) for the rest of this chapter.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.11. \(\F{n}\text{,}\) coordinate.

    \(\F{n}\) is the set of all lists of length \(n\) of elements of \(\F{}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \F{n} = \{ (x_1, \dots, x_n): x_k \in\F{} \ for\ k = 1, \dots, n \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For \((x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \F{n}\) and \(k \in \{1, \dots, n\}\text{,}\) we say that \(x_k\) is the \(k^\nth\) coordinate of \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(\F{} = \R{}\) and \(n\) equals \(2\) or \(3\text{,}\) then the definition above of \(\F{n}\) agrees with our previous notions of \(\R{2}\) and \(\RR{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.12. \(\C{4}\).

    \(\C{4}\) is the set of all lists of four complex numbers:
    \begin{equation*} \C{4} = \{ (z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) : z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4 \in \C{} \}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Read Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions, by Edwin A. Abbott, for an amusing account of how \(\R{3}\) would be perceived by creatures living in \(\RR{2}\text{.}\) This novel, published in 1884, may help you imagine a physical space of four or more dimensions.
    🔗
    If \(n \ge 4\text{,}\) we cannot visualize \(\R{n}\) as a physical object. Similarly, \(\C{1}\) can be thought of as a plane, but for \(n \ge 2\text{,}\) the human brain cannot provide a full image of \(\CC{n}\text{.}\) However, even if \(n\) is large, we can perform algebraic manipulations in \(\F{n}\) as easily as in \(\R{2}\) or \(\RR{3}\text{.}\) For example, addition in \(\F{n}\) is defined as follows.
    🔗

    Definition 1.13. addition in \(\F{n}\).

    Addition in \(\F{n}\) is defined by adding corresponding coordinates:
    \begin{equation*} (x_1, \dots, x_n) + (y_1, \dots, y_n) = (x_1 + y_1, \dots, x_n + y_n). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Often the mathematics of \(\F{n}\) becomes cleaner if we use a single letter to denote a list of \(n\) numbers, without explicitly writing the coordinates. For example, the next result is stated with \(x\) and \(y\) in \(\F{n}\) even though the proof requires the more cumbersome notation of \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\) and \((y_1, \dots, y_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 1.14. commutativity of addition in \(\F{n}\).

    If \(x, y \in \FF{n}\text{,}\) then \(x + y = y + x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \F n\) and \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_n) \in \FF n\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} x + y \amp = (x_1, \dots, x_n) + (y_1, \dots, y_n )\\ \amp = (x_1 + y_1, \dots, x_n + y_n)\\ \amp = (y_1 + x_1, \dots, y_n + x_n)\\ \amp = (y_1, \dots, y_n) + (x_1, \dots, x_n )\\ \amp = y + x\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the second and fourth equalities above hold because of the definition of addition in \(\F{n}\) and the third equality holds because of the usual commutativity of addition in \(\F{}{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The symbol \qedsymbol means “end of proof”.
    🔗
    If a single letter is used to denote an element of \(\FF{n}\text{,}\) then the same letter with appropriate subscripts is often used when coordinates must be displayed. For example, if \(x \in \FF{n}\text{,}\) then letting \(x\) equal \((x_1, \dots, x_n)\) is good notation, as shown in the proof above. Even better, work with just \(x\) and avoid explicit coordinates when possible.
    🔗

    Notation 1.15. \(0\).

    Let \(0\) denote the list of length \(n\) whose coordinates are all \(0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 0 = (0, \dots, 0). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Here we are using the symbol \(0\) in two different ways—on the left side of the equation above, the symbol \(0\) denotes a list of length \(n\text{,}\) which is an element of \(\FF n\text{,}\) whereas on the right side, each \(0\) denotes a number. This potentially confusing practice actually causes no problems because the context should always make clear which \(0\) is intended.
    🔗

    Example 1.16. context determines which \(0\) is intended.

    Consider the statement that \(0\) is an additive identity for \(\F{n}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x + 0 = x \text{ for all } x \in \FF{n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Here the \(0\) above is the list defined in Notation 1.15, not the number \(0\text{,}\) because we have not defined the sum of an element of \(\F{n}\) (namely, \(x\)) and the number \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    
    Missing description
    🔗
    Elements of \(\R{2}\) can be thought of as points or as vectors.
    🔗
    A picture can aid our intuition. We will draw pictures in \(\R{2}\) because we can sketch this space on two-dimensional surfaces such as paper and computer screens. A typical element of \(\R{2}\) is a point \(v = (a, b)\text{.}\) Sometimes we think of \(v\) not as a point but as an arrow starting at the origin and ending at \((a, b)\text{,}\) as shown here. When we think of an element of \(\R{2}\) as an arrow, we refer to it as a vector.
    🔗
    

    🔗
    A vector.
    🔗

With that viewpoint, you will often gain better understanding by dispensing with the coordinate axes and the explicit coordinates and just thinking of the vector, as shown in the figure here.     Aside
    Mathematical models of the economy can have thousands of variables, say \(x_1, \dots, x_{5000}\text{,}\) which means that we must work in \(\R{5000}\text{.}\) Such a space cannot be dealt with geometrically. However, the algebraic approach works well. Thus our subject is called linear algebra.
    🔗
    Whenever we use pictures in \(\R{2}\) or use the somewhat vague language of points and vectors, remember that these are just aids to our understanding, not substitutes for the actual mathematics that we will develop. Although we cannot draw good pictures in high-dimensional spaces, the elements of these spaces are as rigorously defined as elements of \(\RR{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For example, \(( 2, -3, 17, \pi, \sqrt{2} )\) is an element of \(\RR{5}\text{,}\) and we may casually refer to it as a point in \(\R{5}\) or a vector in \(\R{5}\) without worrying about whether the geometry of \(\R{5}\) has any physical meaning.

    Having dealt with addition in \(\FF{n}\text{,}\) we now turn to multiplication. We could define a multiplication in \(\F{n}\) in a similar fashion, starting with two elements of \(\F{n}\) and getting another element of \(\F{n}\) by multiplying corresponding coordinates. Experience shows that this definition is not useful for our purposes. Another type of multiplication, called scalar multiplication, will be central to our subject. Specifically, we need to define what it means to multiply an element of \(\F{n}\) by an element of \(\F{}{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 1.18. scalar multiplication in \(\F{n}\).

    The product of a number \(\lambda\) and a vector in \(\F{n}\) is computed by multiplying each coordinate of the vector by \(\lambda\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lambda (x_1, \dots, x_n) = (\lambda x_1, \dots, \lambda x_n); \end{equation*}
    here \(\lambda \in \F{}\) and \((x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \FF{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Scalar multiplication in \(\F n\) multiplies together a scalar and a vector, getting a vector. In contrast, the dot product in \(\R{2}\) or \(\R{3}\) multiplies together two vectors and gets a scalar. Generalizations of the dot product will become important in Chapter 6.
    🔗
    Scalar multiplication has a nice geometric interpretation in \(\RR{2}\text{.}\) If \(\lambda>0\) and \(x \in \RR{2}\text{,}\) then \(\lambda x\) is the vector that points in the same direction as \(x\) and whose length is \(\lambda\) times the length of \(x\text{.}\) In other words, to get \(\lambda x\text{,}\) we shrink or stretch \(x\) by a factor of \(\lambda\text{,}\) depending on whether \(\lambda \lt 1\) or \(\lambda > 1\text{.}\)

    Subsection 1.1.4 Digression on Fields

    A field is a set containing at least two distinct elements called \(0\) and \(1\text{,}\) along with operations of addition and multiplication satisfying all properties listed in Theorem 1.3. Thus \(\R{}\) and \(\C{}\) are fields, as is the set of rational numbers along with the usual operations of addition and multiplication. Another example of a field is the set \(\{0, 1\}\) with the usual operations of addition and multiplication except that \(1 + 1\) is defined to equal \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In this book we will not deal with fields other than \(\R{}\) and \(\C{}\text{.}\) However, many of the definitions, theorems, and proofs in linear algebra that work for the fields \(\R{}\) and \(\C{}\) also work without change for arbitrary fields. If you prefer to do so, throughout much of this book (except for Chapters 6 and Chapter 7, which deal with inner product spaces) you can think of \(\F{}\) as denoting an arbitrary field instead of \(\R{}\) or \(\C{}\text{.}\) For results (except in the inner product chapters) that have as a hypothesis that \(\F{}\) is \(\C{}\text{,}\) you can probably replace that hypothesis with the hypothesis that \(\F{}\) is an algebraically closed field, which means that every nonconstant polynomial with coefficients in \(\F{}\) has a zero. A few results, such as Exercise 1.3.2.13 in Section 1.3, require the hypothesis on \(\F{}\) that \(1 + 1 \ne 0\text{.}\)
