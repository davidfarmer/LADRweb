    Section 9.4 Tensor Products

    Subsection 9.4.1 Tensor Product of Two Vector Spaces

    The motivation for our next topic comes from wanting to form the product of a vector \(v \in V\) and a vector \(w \in W{}\text{.}\) This product will be denoted by \(v \tensor w\text{,}\) pronounced “\(v\) tensor \(w\)”, and will be an element of some new vector space called \(V{} \tensor W{}\) (also pronounced “\(V\) tensor \(W\)”).
    🔗
    We already have a vector space \(V{} \times W\) (see Section 3.5), called the product of \(V\) and \(W{}\text{.}\) However, \(V{} \times W\) will not serve our purposes here because it does not provide a natural way to multiply an element of \(V\) by an element of \(W{}\text{.}\) We would like our tensor product to satisfy some of the usual properties of multiplication. For example, we would like the distributive property to be satisfied, meaning that if \(v_1, v_2, v \in V\) and \(w_1, w_2, w \in W{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} (v_1 + v_2) \tensor w = v_1 \tensor w + v_2 \tensor w \andd v \tensor (w_1 + w_2) = v \tensor w_1 + v \tensor w_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Aside
    To produce \(\tensor\) in TeX, type \otimes.
    🔗
    We would also like scalar multiplication to interact well with this new multiplication, meaning that
    \begin{equation*} \lambda (v \tensor w) = (\lambda v) \tensor w = v \tensor (\lambda w) \end{equation*}
    for all \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) and \(w \in W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Furthermore, it would be nice if each basis of \(V\) when combined with each basis of \(W\) produced a basis of \(V{} \tensor W{}\text{.}\) Specifically, if \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) is a basis of \(V\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) is a basis of \(W{}\text{,}\) then we would like a list (in any order) consisting of \(e_j \tensor f_k\text{,}\) as \(j\) ranges from \(1\) to \(m\) and \(k\) ranges from \(1\) to \(n\text{,}\) to be a basis of \(V{} \tensor W{}\text{.}\) This implies that \(\dim (V{} \tensor W)\) should equal \((\dim V)(\dim W)\text{.}\) Recall that \(\dim(V{} \times W) = \dim V + \dim W\) (see 3.92), which shows that the product \(V{} \times W\) will not serve our purposes here.
    🔗
    To produce a vector space whose dimension is \((\dim V)(\dim W)\) in a natural fashion from \(V\) and \(W\text{,}\) we look at the vector space of bilinear functionals, as defined below.
    🔗

    Definition 9.68. bilinear functional on \(V \times W\text{,}\) the vector space \(\B(V,W)\).

    1. A bilinear functional on \(V{} \times W\) is a function \(\fun \beta {V{} \times W} {\F{}}\) such that \(v \mapsto \beta(v, w)\) is a linear functional on \(V\) for each \(w\in W\) and \(w \mapsto \beta(v, w)\) is a linear functional on \(W\) for each \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The vector space of bilinear functionals on \(V{} \times W\) is denoted by \(\B(V{},W)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(W = V\text{,}\) then a bilinear functional on \(V \times W\) is a bilinear form; see 9.1.
    🔗
    The operations of addition and scalar multiplication on \(\B(V{}, W)\) are defined to be the usual operations of addition and scalar multiplication of functions. As you can verify, these operations make \(\B(V{},W)\) into a vector space whose additive identity is the zero function from \(V \times W\) to \(\F{}{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 9.69. bilinear functionals.

    1. Suppose \(\phi \in V'\) and \(\tau \in W'\!\text{.}\) Define \(\fun \beta {V{} \times W} {\F{}}\) by \(\beta(v, w) = \phi(v) \tau(w)\text{.}\) Then \(\beta\) is a bilinear functional on \(V{} \times W{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\text{.}\) Define \(\fun \beta {V'\! \times W'} {\F{}}\) by \(\beta(\phi, \tau) = \phi(v) \tau(w)\text{.}\) Then \(\beta\) is a bilinear functional on \(V'\! \times W'\!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Define \(\fun \beta {V{} \times V'} {\F{}}\) by \(\beta(v, \phi) = \phi(v)\text{.}\) Then \(\beta\) is a bilinear functional on \(V{} \times V'\!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Suppose \(\phi \in V'\!\text{.}\) Define \(\fun \beta {V{} \times \Lc(V)} {\F{}}\) by \(\beta(v, T) = \phi(Tv)\text{.}\) Then \(\beta\) is a bilinear functional on \(V{} \times \Lc(V)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Suppose \(m\) and \(n\) are positive integers. Define \(\fun \beta { \F{m, n} \times \F{n,m} } {\F{}}\) by \(\beta(A, B) = \tr(AB)\text{.}\) Then \(\beta\) is a bilinear functional on \(\F{m, n} \times \F{n,m}\!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 9.70. dimension of the vector space of bilinear functionals.

    \(\dim \B(V{},W) = (\dim V)(\dim W)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) be a basis of \(V\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) be a basis of \(W{}\text{.}\) For a bilinear functional \(\beta \in \B(V{},W)\text{,}\) let \(\M(\beta)\) be the \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix whose entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\) is \(\beta(e_j, f_k)\text{.}\) The map \(\beta \mapsto \M(\beta)\) is a linear map of \(\B(V{},W)\) into \(\FF{m,n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For a matrix \(C \in \FF{m,n}\text{,}\) define a bilinear functional \(\beta_C\) on \(V{} \times W\) by
    \begin{equation*} \beta_C( a_1 e_1+ \cdots + a_m e_m, b_1 f_1 + \cdots + b_n f_n ) = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m C_{j,k} a_j b_k \end{equation*}
    for \(a_1, \ldots, a_m, b_1, \ldots, b_n \in \F{}{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The linear map \(\beta \mapsto \M(\beta)\) from \(\B(V{}, W)\) to \(\F{m,n}\) and the linear map \(C \mapsto \beta_C\) from \(\F{m,n}\) to \(\B(V{},W)\) are inverses of each other because \(\beta_{\M(\beta)} = \beta\) for all \(\beta \in \B(V{}, W)\) and \(\M(\beta_C) = C\) for all \(C \in \FF{m,n}\text{,}\) as you should verify.
    🔗
    Thus both maps are isomorphisms and the two spaces that they connect have the same dimension. Hence \(\dim \B(V{},W)= \dim \F{m,n} = mn = (\dim V)(\dim W)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Several different definitions of \(V{} \tensor W\) appear in the mathematical literature. These definitions are equivalent to each other, at least in the finite-dimensional context, because any two vector spaces of the same dimension are isomorphic.
    🔗
    The result above states that \(\B(V{}, W)\) has the dimension that we seek, as do \(\Lc(V{}, W)\) and \(\FF{\dim V{}, \dim W}\text{.}\) Thus it may be tempting to define \(V{} \tensor W\) to be \(\B(V{}, W)\) or \(\Lc(V{}, W)\) or \(\FF{\dim V{}, \dim W}\text{.}\) However, none of those definitions would lead to a basis-free definition of \(v \tensor w\) for \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The following definition, while it may seem a bit strange and abstract at first, has the huge advantage that it defines \(v \tensor w\) in a basis-free fashion. We define \(V{} \tensor W\) to be the vector space of bilinear functionals on \(V'\! \times W'\) instead of the more tempting choice of the vector space of bilinear functionals on \(V{} \times W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 9.71. tensor product, \(V \tensor W\text{,}\) \(v \tensor w\).

    1. The tensor product \(V{} \tensor W\) is defined to be \(\B( V', W' )\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\text{,}\) the tensor product \(v \tensor w\) is the element of \(V{} \tensor W\) defined by
      \begin{equation*} (v \tensor w)(\phi, \tau) = \phi(v) \tau(w) \end{equation*}
      for all \((\phi, \tau) \in V'\! \times W'\!\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We can quickly prove that the definition of \(V{} \tensor W\) gives it the desired dimension.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.72. dimension of the tensor product of two vector spaces.

    \(\dim (V{} \tensor W) = (\dim V)(\dim W)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Because a vector space and its dual have the same dimension (by Theorem 3.111), we have \(\dim V' = \dim V\) and \(\dim W' = \dim W{}\text{.}\) Thus 9.70 tells us that the dimension of \(\B( V', W' )\) equals \((\dim V)(\dim W)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    To understand the definition of the tensor product \(v \tensor w\) of two vectors \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\text{,}\) focus on the kind of object it is. An element of \(V{} \tensor W\) is a bilinear functional on \(V'\! \times W'\!\text{,}\) and in particular it is a function from \(V'\! \times W'\) to \(\F{}{}\text{.}\) Thus for each element of \(V'\! \times W'\!\text{,}\) it should produce an element of \(\F{}{}\text{.}\) The definition above has this behavior, because \(v \tensor w\) applied to a typical element \((\phi, \tau)\) of \(V'\! \times W'\) produces the number \(\phi(v) \tau(w)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The somewhat abstract nature of \(v \tensor w\) should not matter. The important point is the behavior of these objects. The next result shows that tensor products of vectors have the desired bilinearity properties.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.73. bilinearity of tensor product.

    Suppose \(v, v_1, v_2 \in V\) and \(w, w_1, w_2 \in W\) and \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} (v_1 + v_2) \tensor w = v_1 \tensor w + v_2 \tensor w \andd v \tensor (w_1 + w_2) = v \tensor w_1 + v \tensor w_2 \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \lambda(v \tensor w) = (\lambda v) \tensor w = v \tensor (\lambda w). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \((\phi, \tau) \in V'\! \times W'\!\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} ( (v_1 + v_2) \tensor w )(\phi, \tau) \amp = \phi( v_1 + v_2) \tau(w)\\ \amp = \phi(v_1) \tau(w) + \phi(v_2) \tau(w)\\ \amp = (v_1 \tensor w)(\phi, \tau) + (v_2 \tensor w)(\phi, \tau)\\ \amp = (v_1 \tensor w + v_2 \tensor w)(\phi, \tau)\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus \((v_1 + v_2) \tensor w = v_1 \tensor w + v_2 \tensor w\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The other two equalities are proved similarly.
    🔗
    🔗
    Lists are, by definition, ordered. The order matters when, for example, we form the matrix of an operator with respect to a basis. For lists in this section with two indices, such as \(\{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) in the next result, the ordering does not matter and we do not specify it—just choose any convenient ordering.
    🔗
    The linear independence of elements of \(V{} \tensor W\) in (a) of the result below captures the idea that there are no relationships among vectors in \(V{} \tensor W\) other than the relationships that come from bilinearity of the tensor product (see 9.73) and the relationships that may be present due to linear dependence of a list of vectors in \(V\) or a list of vectors in \(W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 9.74. basis of \(V \tensor W\).

    Suppose \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) is a list of vectors in \(V\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) is a list of vectors in \(W{}\text{.}\)
    1. If \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) are both linearly independent lists, then
      \begin{equation*} \{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n} \end{equation*}
      is a linearly independent list in \(V{} \tensor W{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) is a basis of \(V\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) is a basis of \(W{}\text{,}\) then the list \(\{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) is a basis of \(V{} \tensor W{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    To prove (a), suppose \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) are both linearly independent lists. This linear independence and the linear map lemma Theorem 3.4 imply that there exist \(\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_m \in V'\) and \(\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_n \in W'\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \phi_j(e_k) = \begin{cases}1 \amp \text{ if } j = k,\\ 0 \amp \text{ if } j \ne k \end{cases} \andd \tau_j(f_k) = \begin{cases}1 \amp \text{ if } j = k,\\ 0 \amp \text{ if } j \ne k, \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    where \(j, k \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\) in the first equation and \(j, k \in \{ 1, \ldots, n \}\) in the second equation.
    🔗
    Suppose \(\{ a_{j,k} \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) is a list of scalars such that
    \begin{equation} \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m a_{j,k} (e_j \tensor f_k) = 0.\tag{9.75} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Note that \((e_j \tensor f_k)(\phi_M, \tau_N)\) equals \(1\) if \(j=M\) and \(k = N\text{,}\) and equals \(0\) otherwise. Thus applying both sides of (9.75) to \((\phi_M, \tau_N)\) shows that \(a_{M,N} = 0\text{,}\) proving that \(\{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) is linearly independent.
    🔗
    Now (b) follows from (a), the equation \(\dim V{} \tensor W = (\dim V)(\dim W)\) [see 9.72], and the result that a linearly independent list of the right length is a basis (see 2.38).
    🔗
    🔗
    Every element of \(V{} \tensor W\) is a finite sum of elements of the form \(v \tensor w\text{,}\) where \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\text{,}\) as implied by (b) in the result above. However, if \(\dim V > 1\) and \(\dim W > 1\text{,}\) then Exercise 9.4.3.4 shows that
    \begin{equation*} \{ v \tensor w : (v, w) \in V{} \times W \} \ne V{} \tensor W{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 9.76. tensor product of element of \(\F m\) with element of \(\F n\).

    Suppose \(m\) and \(n\) are positive integers. Let \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) denote the standard basis of \(\F m\) and let \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) denote the standard basis of \(\FF n\text{.}\) Suppose
    \begin{equation*} v = (v_1, \ldots, v_m) \in \F m \andd w = (w_1, \ldots, w_n) \in \F n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then
    \begin{align*} v \tensor w \amp = ( \sum_{j=1}^m v_j e_j ) \tensor ( \sum_{k=1}^n w_k f_k )\\ \amp = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m (v_j w_k) ( e_j \tensor f_k )\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus with respect to the basis \(\{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) of \(\F m \tensor \F n\) provided by Theorem 9.74(b), the coefficients of \(v \tensor w\) are the numbers \(\{ v_j w_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\text{.}\) If instead of writing these numbers in a list, we write them in an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix with \(v_j w_k\) in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) then we can identify \(v \tensor w\) with the \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}v_1 w_1 \amp \cdots \amp v_1 w_n \\ \amp \ddots \amp \\ v_m w_1 \amp \cdots \amp v_m w_n \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    See Exercises 9.4.3.5 and Exercise 9.4.3.6 for practice in using the identification from the example above.
    🔗
    We now define bilinear maps, which differ from bilinear functionals in that the target space can be an arbitrary vector space rather than just the scalar field.
    🔗

    Definition 9.77. bilinear map.

    A bilinear map from \(V{} \times W\) to a vector space \(U\) is a function \(\fun \Gamma {V{} \times W} U\) such that \(v \mapsto \Gamma(v, w)\) is a linear map from \(V\) to \(U\) for each \(w\in W\) and \(w \mapsto \Gamma(v, w)\) is a linear map from \(W\) to \(U\) for each \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.78. bilinear maps.

    1. Every bilinear functional on \(V{} \times W\) is a bilinear map from \(V{} \times W\) to \(\F{}{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The function \(\fun \Gamma {V{} \times W} {V{} \tensor W}\) defined by \(\Gamma(v, w) = v \tensor w\) is a bilinear map from \(V{} \times W\) to \(V{} \tensor W\) (by Theorem 9.73).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The function \(\fun \Gamma {\Lc(V) \times \Lc(V)} {\Lc(V)}\) defined by \(\Gamma(S, T) = ST\) is a bilinear map from \(\Lc(V) \times \Lc(V)\) to \(\Lc(V)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The function \(\fun \Gamma {V{} \times \Lc(V{}, W)} W\) defined by \(\Gamma(v, T) = Tv\) is a bilinear map from \(V{} \times \Lc(V{}, W)\) to \(W{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Tensor products allow us to convert bilinear maps on \(V{} \times W\) into linear maps on \(V{} \tensor W\) (and vice versa), as shown by the next result. In the mathematical literature, (a) of the result below is called the “universal property” of tensor products.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.79. converting bilinear maps to linear maps.

    Suppose \(U\) is a vector space.
    1. Suppose \(\fun \Gamma {V{} \times W} U\) is a bilinear map. Then there exists a unique linear map \(\fun {\hat{\Gamma}} {V{} \tensor W} U\) such that
      \begin{equation*} \hat{\Gamma}(v \tensor w) = \Gamma(v, w) \end{equation*}
      for all \((v, w) \in V{} \times W{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Conversely, suppose \(\fun T {V{} \tensor W} U\) is a linear map. Then there exists a unique bilinear map \(\fun {\pound{T}} {V{} \times W} U\) such that
      \begin{equation*} \pound{T}(v, w) = T(v \tensor w) \end{equation*}
      for all \((v, w) \in V{} \times W{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) be a basis of \(V\) and let \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) be a basis of \(W{}\text{.}\) By the linear map lemma Theorem 3.4 and Theorem 9.74(b), there exists a unique linear map \(\fun {\hat{\Gamma}} {V{} \tensor W} U\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \hat{\Gamma}(e_j \tensor f_k) = \Gamma(e_j, f_k) \end{equation*}
    for all \(j \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\) and \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, n \}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now suppose \((v, w) \in V{} \times W{}\text{.}\) There exist \(a_1, \ldots, a_m, b_1, \ldots, b_n \in \F{}\) such that \(v = a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_m e_m\) and \(w = b_1 f_1 + \cdots + b_n f_n\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{align*} \hat{\Gamma}(v \tensor w) \amp = \hat{\Gamma} ( \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m (a_j b_k) (e_j \tensor f_k) )\\ \amp = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m a_j b_k \hat{\Gamma}(e_j \tensor f_k)\\ \amp = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m a_j b_k \Gamma(e_j , f_k)\\ \amp = \Gamma(v, w)\text{,} \end{align*}
    as desired, where the second line holds because \(\hat{\Gamma}\) is linear, the third line holds by the definition of \(\hat{\Gamma}\text{,}\) and the fourth line holds because \(\Gamma\) is bilinear.
    🔗
    The uniqueness of the linear map \(\hat{\Gamma}\) satisfying \(\hat{\Gamma}(v \tensor w) = \Gamma(v, w)\) follows from Theorem 9.74(b), completing the proof of (a).
    🔗
    To prove (b), define a function \(\fun {\pound{T}} {V{} \times W} U\) by \(\pound{T}(v, w) = T(v \tensor w)\) for all \((v, w) \in V{} \times W{}\text{.}\) The bilinearity of the tensor product (see 9.73) and the linearity of \(T\) imply that \(\pound{T}\) is bilinear.
    🔗
    Clearly the choice of \(\pound{T}\) that satisfies the conditions is unique.
    🔗
    🔗
    To prove 9.79(a), we could not just define \(\hat{\Gamma}(v \tensor w) = \Gamma(v, w)\) for all \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W\) \big(and then extend \(\hat{\Gamma}\) linearly to all of \(V{} \tensor W\)\big) because elements of \(V{} \tensor W\) do not have unique representations as finite sums of elements of the form \(v \tensor w\text{.}\) Our proof used a basis of \(V\) and a basis of \(W\) to get around this problem.
    🔗
    Although our construction of \(\hat{\Gamma}\) in the proof of Theorem 9.79(a) depended on a basis of \(V\) and a basis of \(W{}\text{,}\) the equation \(\hat{\Gamma}(v \tensor w) = \Gamma(v, w)\) that holds for all \(v \in V\) and \(w \in W{}\) shows that \(\hat{\Gamma}\) does not depend on the choice of bases for \(V\) and \(W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 9.4.2 Tensor Product of Inner Product Spaces

    The result below features three inner products—one on \(V{} \tensor W{}\text{,}\) one on \(V{}\text{,}\) and one on \(W{}\text{,}\) although we use the same symbol \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\) for all three inner products.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.80. inner product on tensor product of two inner product spaces.

    Suppose \(V\) and \(W\) are inner product spaces. Then there is a unique inner product on \(V{} \tensor W\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \langle v \tensor w, u \tensor x \rangle = \langle v, u \rangle \langle w, x \rangle \end{equation*}
    for all \(v, u \in V\) and \(w, x \in W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(W{}\text{.}\) Define an inner product on \(V{} \tensor W\) by
    \begin{equation} \langle \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m b_{j,k} e_j \tensor f_k, \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m c_{j,k} e_j \tensor f_k \rangle = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m b_{j,k} \overline{ c_{j,k} }.\tag{9.81} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The straightforward verification that (9.81) defines an inner product on \(V{} \tensor W\) is left to the reader [use 9.74(b)] .
    🔗
    Suppose that \(v, u \in V\) and \(w, x \in W{}\text{.}\) Let \(v_1, \ldots, v_m \in \F{}\) be such that \(v = v_1 e_1 + \cdots + v_m e_m\text{,}\) with similar expressions for \(u\text{,}\) \(w\text{,}\) and \(x\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \langle v \tensor w, u \tensor x \rangle \amp = \langle \sum_{j=1}^m v_j e_j \tensor \sum_{k=1}^n w_k f_k , \sum_{j=1}^m u_j e_j \tensor \sum_{k=1}^n x_k f_k \rangle\\ \amp = \langle \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m v_j w_k e_j \tensor f_k , \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m u_j x_k e_j \tensor f_k \rangle\\ \amp = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m v_j\overline{u_j} w_k \overline{x_k}\\ \amp = ( \sum_{j=1}^m v_j \overline{u_j} ) ( \sum_{k=1}^n w_k \overline{x_k} )\\ \amp = \langle v, u \rangle \langle w, x \rangle \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    There is only one inner product on \(V{} \tensor W\) such that \(\langle v \tensor w, u \tensor x \rangle = \langle v, u \rangle \langle w, x \rangle\) for all \(v, u \in V\) and \(w, x \in W\) because every element of \(V{} \tensor W\) can be written as a linear combination of elements of the form \(v \tensor w\) [by Theorem 9.74(b)] .
    🔗
    🔗
    The definition below of a natural inner product on \(V{} \tensor W\) is now justified by Theorem 9.80. We could not have simply defined \(\langle v \tensor w, u \tensor x \rangle\) to be \(\langle v, u \rangle \langle w, x \rangle\) (and then used additivity in each slot separately to extend the definition to \(V{} \tensor W\)) without some proof because elements of \(V{} \tensor W\) do not have unique representations as finite sums of elements of the form \(v \tensor w\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 9.82. inner product on tensor product of two inner product spaces.

    Suppose \(V\) and \(W\) are inner product spaces. The inner product on \(V{} \tensor W\) is the unique function \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\) from \((V{} \tensor W) \times (V{} \tensor W)\) to \(\F{}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \langle v \tensor w, u \tensor x \rangle = \langle v, u \rangle \langle w, x \rangle \end{equation*}
    for all \(v, u \in V\) and \(w, x \in W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Take \(u = v\) and \(x = w\) in the equation above and then take square roots to show that
    \begin{equation*} \lVert v \tensor w \rVert = \lVert v \rVert \lVert w \rVert \end{equation*}
    for all \(v \in V\) and all \(w \in W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The construction of the inner product in the proof of Theorem 9.80 depended on an orthonormal basis \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) of \(V\) and an orthonormal basis \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) of \(W{}\text{.}\) Formula (9.81) implies that \(\{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) is a doubly indexed orthonormal list in \(V{} \tensor W\) and hence is an orthonormal basis of \(V{} \tensor W\) [by Theorem 9.74(b)] . The importance of the next result arises because the orthonormal bases used there can be different from the orthonormal bases used to define the inner product in Theorem 9.80. Although the notation for the bases is the same in the proof of Theorem 9.80 and in the result below, think of them as two different sets of orthonormal bases.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.83. orthonormal basis of \(V \tensor W\).

    Suppose \(V\) and \(W\) are inner product spaces, and \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(W{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n} \end{equation*}
    is an orthonormal basis of \(V{} \tensor W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We know that \(\{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) is a basis of \(V{} \tensor W\) [by Theorem 9.74(b)] . Thus we only need to verify orthonormality. To do this, suppose \(j, M \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\) and \(k, N \in \{ 1, \ldots, n \}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \langle e_j \tensor f_k, e_M \tensor f_N \rangle = \langle e_j, e_M \rangle \langle f_k, f_N \rangle = \begin{cases}1 \amp \text{ if } j = M \text{ and } k = N, \\ 0 \amp \text{ otherwise } . \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence the doubly indexed list \(\{ e_j \tensor f_k \}_{j = 1, \ldots, m; k = 1, \ldots, n}\) is indeed an orthonormal basis of \(V{} \tensor W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    See Exercise 9.4.3.11 for an example of how the inner product structure on \(V{} \tensor W\) interacts with operators on \(V\) and \(W{}\text{.}\)

    Subsection 9.4.3 Tensor Product of Multiple Vector Spaces

    We have been discussing properties of the tensor product of two finite-dimensional vector spaces. Now we turn our attention to the tensor product of multiple finite-dimensional vector spaces. This generalization requires no new ideas, only some slightly more complicated notation. Readers with a good understanding of the tensor product of two vector spaces should be able to make the extension to the tensor product of more than two vector spaces.
    🔗
    Thus in this subsection, no proofs will be provided. The definitions and the statements of results that will be provided should be enough information to enable readers to fill in the details, using what has already been learned about the tensor product of two vector spaces.
    🔗
    We begin with the following notational assumption.
    🔗

    Notation 9.84. \(V_1, \ldots, V_m\).

    For the rest of this subsection, \(m\) denotes an integer greater than \(1\) and \(V{}_1, \ldots, V{}_m\) denote finite-dimensional vector spaces.
    🔗
    🔗
    The notion of an \(m\)-linear functional, which we are about to define, generalizes the notion of a bilinear functional (see 9.68). Recall that the use of the word “functional” indicates that we are mapping into the scalar field \(\F{}{}\text{.}\) Recall also that the terminology “\(m\)-linear form” is used in the special case \(V{}_1 = \cdots = V{}_m\) (see 9.25). The notation \(\B(V{}_1, \ldots, V{}_m)\) generalizes our previous notation \(\B(V{}, W)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 9.85. \(m\)-linear functional, the vector space \(\B(V_1, \ldots, V_m)\).

    1. An \(m\)-linear functional on \(V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m\) is a function \(\fun \beta {V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m} {\F{}}\) that is a linear functional in each slot when the other slots are held fixed.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The vector space of \(m\)-linear functionals on \(V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m\) is denoted by \(\B(V{}_1, \ldots, V{}_m)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 9.86. \(m\)-linear functional.

    Suppose \(\phi_k \in {V{}_k}'\) for each \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\text{.}\) Define \(\fun \beta {V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m} {\F{}}\) by
    \begin{equation*} \beta(v_1, \ldots, v_m) = \phi_1(v_1) \times \cdots \times \phi_m(v_m). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then \(\beta\) is an \(m\)-linear functional on \(V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result can be proved by imitating the proof of Theorem 9.70.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.87. dimension of the vector space of \(m\)-linear functionals.

    \(\dim \B(V{}_1, \ldots, V{}_m) = (\dim V{}_1) \times \cdots \times (\dim V{}_m)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Now we can define the tensor product of multiple vector spaces and the tensor product of elements of those vector spaces. The following definition is completely analogous to our previous definition Definition 9.71 in the case \(m = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 9.88. tensor product, \(V_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V_m\text{,}\) \(v_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor v_m\).

    1. The tensor product \(V{}_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V{}_m\) is defined to be \(\B( V{}_1\!', \ldots, V{}_m\!' )\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For \(v_1 \in V{}_1, \ldots, v_m \in V{}_m\text{,}\) the tensor product \(v_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor v_m\) is the element of \(V{}_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V{}_m\) defined by
      \begin{equation*} (v_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor v_m)(\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_m) = \phi_1(v_1) \cdots \phi_m(v_m) \end{equation*}
      for all \((\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_m) \in V{}_1\!' \times \cdots \times V{}_m\!'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result can be proved by following the pattern of the proof of the analogous result when \(m = 2\) (see 9.72).
    🔗

    Theorem 9.89. dimension of the tensor product.

    \(\dim (V{}_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V{}_m) = (\dim V{}_1) \cdots (\dim V{}_m)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Our next result generalizes 9.74.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.90. basis of \(V_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V_m\).

    Suppose \(\dim V{}_k = n_k\) and \(e_1^{ k}, \ldots, e_{n_k}^{ k}\) is a basis of \(V{}_k\) for \(k = 1, \ldots, m\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \{ e_{j_1}^1 \tensor \cdots \tensor e_{j_m}^m \}_{j_1 = 1, \ldots, n_1; \cdots ; j_m = 1, \ldots, n_m} \end{equation*}
    is a basis of \(V{}_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V{}_m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose \(m = 2\) and \(e_1^1, \ldots, e_{n_1}^1\) is a basis of \(V{}_1\) and \(e_1^2, \ldots, e_{n_2}^2\) is a basis of \(V{}_2\text{.}\) Then with respect to the basis \(\{ e_{j_1}^1 \tensor e_{j_2}^2 \}_{j_1 = 1, \ldots, n_1; j_2 = 1, \ldots, n_2}\) in the result above, the coefficients of an element of \(V{}_1 \tensor V{}_2\) can be represented by an \(n_1\)-by-\(n_2\) matrix that contains the coefficient of \(e_{j_1}^1 \tensor e_{j_2}^2\) in row \(j_1\text{,}\) column \(j_2\text{.}\) Thus we need a matrix, which is an array specified by two indices, to represent an element of \(V{}_1 \tensor V{}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(m > 2\text{,}\) then the result above shows that we need an array specified by \(m\) indices to represent an arbitrary element of \(V{}_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V{}_m\text{.}\) Thus tensor products may appear when we deal with objects specified by arrays with multiple indices.
    🔗
    The next definition generalizes the notion of a bilinear map (see 9.77). As with bilinear maps, the target space can be an arbitrary vector space.
    🔗

    Definition 9.91. \(m\)-linear map.

    An \(m\)-linear map from \(V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m\) to a vector space \(U\) is a function \(\fun \Gamma {V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m} U\) that is a linear map in each slot when the other slots are held fixed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result can be proved by following the pattern of the proof of Theorem 9.79.
    🔗

    Theorem 9.92. converting \(m\)-linear maps to linear maps.

    Suppose \(U\) is a vector space.
    1. Suppose that \(\fun \Gamma {V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m} U\) is an \(m\)-linear map. Then there exists a unique linear map \(\fun {\hat{\Gamma}} {V{}_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V{}_m} U\) such that
      \begin{equation*} \hat{\Gamma}(v_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor v_m) = \Gamma(v_1, \ldots, v_m) \end{equation*}
      for all \((v_1, \ldots, v_m) \in V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Conversely, suppose \(\fun T {V{}_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor V{}_m} U\) is a linear map. Then there exists a unique \(m\)-linear map \(\fun {\pound{T}} {V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m} U\) such that
      \begin{equation*} \pound{T}(v_1, \ldots, v_m) = T(v_1 \tensor \cdots \tensor v_m) \end{equation*}
      for all \((v_1, \ldots, v_m) \in V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    for all \((v_1, \ldots, v_m) \in V{}_1 \times \cdots \times V{}_m\text{.}\)
