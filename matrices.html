    Section 3.3 Matrices

    Subsection 3.3.1 Representing a Linear Map by a Matrix

    We know that if \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) is a basis of \(V\) and \(T \colon V \rightarrow W\) is linear, then the values of \(Tv_1, \dots, Tv_n\) determine the values of \(T\) on arbitrary vectors in \(V\)—see the linear map lemma Theorem 3.4. As we will soon see, matrices provide an efficient method of recording the values of the \(Tv_k\)’s in terms of a basis of \(W{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 3.27. matrix, \(A_{j,k}\).

    Suppose \(m\) and \(n\) are nonnegative integers. An \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix \(A\) is a rectangular array of elements of \(\F{}\) with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns:
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix}A_{1,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{1,n} \\ \vdots \amp \amp \vdots \\ A_{m,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{m,n} \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The notation \(A_{j,k}\) denotes the entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\) of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.28. \(A_{j,k}\) equals entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\) of \(A\).

    Aside
    When dealing with matrices, the first index refers to the row number; the second index refers to the column number.
    🔗
    Suppose \(A = \begin{pmatrix}8 \amp 4 \amp 5-3i\\ 1 \amp 9 \amp 7 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\) Thus \(A_{2,3}\) refers to the entry in the second row, third column of \(A\text{,}\) which means that \(A_{2,3} = 7\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Now we come to the key definition in this section.
    🔗

    Definition 3.29. matrix of a linear map, \(\M(T)\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\) and \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) is a basis of \(V\) and \(w_1, \dots, w_m\) is a basis of \(W{}\text{.}\) The matrix of \(T\) with respect to these bases is the \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix \(\M(T)\) whose entries \(A_{j,k}\) are defined by
    \begin{equation*} Tv_k = A_{1,k} w_1 + \dots + A_{m,k} w_m. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If the bases \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) and \(w_1, \dots, w_m\) are not clear from the context, then the notation \(\M\bigl( T, (v_1, \dots, v_n), (w_1, \dots, w_m) \bigr)\) is used.
    🔗
    🔗
    The matrix \(\M(T)\) of a linear map \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\) depends on the basis \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) of \(V\) and the basis \(w_1, \dots, w_m\) of \(W{}\text{,}\) as well as on \(T{}\text{.}\) However, the bases should be clear from the context, and thus they are often not included in the notation.
    🔗
    To remember how \(\M(T)\) is constructed from \(T\text{,}\) you might write across the top of the matrix the basis vectors \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) for the domain and along the left the basis vectors \(w_1, \dots, w_m\) for the vector space into which \(T\) maps, as follows:
    \begin{align*} v_1 \cdots v_k \cdots v_n \begin{array}{c} w_1 \\ \M(T) =\ \vdots \\ w_m \end{array} \begin{pmatrix}\amp \amp \amp \amp A_{1,k} \amp \amp \amp \amp \\ \amp \amp \amp \amp \vdots \amp \amp \amp \amp \\ \amp \amp \amp \amp A_{m,k} \amp \amp \amp \amp \end{pmatrix} \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Aside
    The \(k^\nth\) column of  \(\M(T)\) consists of the scalars needed to write \(Tv_k\) as a linear combination of \(w_1, \dots, w_m\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} Tv_k = \sum_{j=1}^m A_{j,k} w_j. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In the matrix above only the \(k^\nth\) column is shown. Thus the second index of each displayed entry of the matrix above is \(k\text{.}\) The picture above should remind you that \(Tv_k\) can be computed from \(\M(T)\) by multiplying each entry in the \(k^\nth\) column by the corresponding \(w_j\) from the left column, and then adding up the resulting vectors.
    🔗

    Aside
    If \(T\) is a linear map from an \(n\)-dimensional vector space to an \(m\)-dimensional vector space, then \(\M(T)\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix.
    🔗
    If \(T\) is a linear map from \(\F{n}\) to \(\FF{m}\text{,}\) then unless stated otherwise, assume the bases in question are the standard ones (where the \(k^\nth\) basis vector is \(1\) in the \(k^\nth\) slot and \(0\) in all other slots). If you think of elements of \(\F{m}\) as columns of \(m\) numbers, then you can think of the \(k^\nth\) column of \(\M(T)\) as \(T\) applied to the \(k^\nth\) standard basis vector.
    🔗

    Example 3.30. the matrix of a linear map from \(\F2\) to \(\F3\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc( \FF{2}, \F{3} )\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} T(x, y) = (x + 3y, 2x + 5y, 7x + 9y). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because \(T(1,0) = (1, 2, 7)\) and \(T(0,1) = (3, 5, 9)\text{,}\) the matrix of \(T\) with respect to the standard bases is the \(3\)-by-\(2\) matrix below:
    \begin{equation*} \M(T) = \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 5 \\ 7 \amp 9 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    When working with \(\P{}_m(\F{})\text{,}\) use the standard basis \(1, x, x^2{}, \dots, x^m\) unless the context indicates otherwise.
    🔗

    Example 3.31. matrix of the differentiation map from \(\P_3(\R)\) to \(\P_2(\R)\).

    Suppose \(D \in \Lc\bigl(\P{}_3(\R{}), \P{}_2(\R{}) \bigr)\) is the differentiation map defined by \(Dp = p'\!\text{.}\) Because \({(x^n)}' = nx^{n-1}\!\text{,}\) the matrix of \(D\) with respect to the standard bases is the \(3\)-by-\(4\) matrix below:
    \begin{equation*} \M(D) = \begin{pmatrix}0\amp 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 3 \\ \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.3.2 Addition and Scalar Multiplication of Matrices

    For the rest of this section, assume that \(U\text{,}\) \(V\text{,}\) and \(W\) are finite-dimensional and that a basis has been chosen for each of these vector spaces. Thus for each linear map from \(V\) to \(W{}\text{,}\) we can talk about its matrix (with respect to the chosen bases).
    🔗
    Is the matrix of the sum of two linear maps equal to the sum of the matrices of the two maps? Right now this question does not yet make sense because although we have defined the sum of two linear maps, we have not defined the sum of two matrices. Fortunately, the natural definition of the sum of two matrices has the right properties. Specifically, we make the following definition.
    🔗

    Definition 3.32. matrix addition.

    The sum of two matrices of the same size is the matrix obtained by adding corresponding entries in the matrices:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}A_{1,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{1,n} \\ \vdots \amp \amp \vdots \\ A_{m,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{m,n} \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}C_{1,1} \amp \cdots \amp C_{1,n} \\ \vdots \amp \amp \vdots \\ C_{m,1} \amp \cdots \amp C_{m,n} \end{pmatrix} \\ = \begin{pmatrix}A_{1,1} + C_{1,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{1,n} + C_{1,n} \\ \vdots \amp \amp \vdots \\ A_{m,1} + C_{m,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{m,n} + C_{m,n} \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In the next result, the assumption is that the same bases are used for all three linear maps \(S+T\text{,}\) \(S\text{,}\) and \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.33. matrix of the sum of linear maps.

    Suppose \(S, T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then \(\M(S + T) = \M(S) + \M(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The verification of the result above follows from the definitions and is left to the reader.
    🔗
    Still assuming that we have some bases in mind, is the matrix of a scalar times a linear map equal to the scalar times the matrix of the linear map? Again, the question does not yet make sense because we have not defined scalar multiplication on matrices. Fortunately, the natural definition again has the right properties.
    🔗

    Definition 3.34. scalar multiplication of a matrix.

    The product of a scalar and a matrix is the matrix obtained by multiplying each entry in the matrix by the scalar:
    \begin{equation*} \lambda \begin{pmatrix}A_{1,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{1,n} \\ \vdots \amp \amp \vdots \\ A_{m,1} \amp \cdots \amp A_{m,n} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda A_{1,1} \amp \cdots \amp \lambda A_{1,n} \\ \vdots \amp \amp \vdots \\ \lambda A_{m,1} \amp \cdots \amp \lambda A_{m,n} \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.35. addition and scalar multiplication of matrices.

    \begin{equation*} 2 \begin{pmatrix}3 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 5 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}4 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}6 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 10 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}4 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}10 \amp 4 \\ -1 \amp 16 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In the next result, the assumption is that the same bases are used for both the linear maps \(\lambda T\) and \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.36. the matrix of a scalar times a linear map.

    Suppose \(\lambda \in \F{}\) and \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then \(\M(\lambda T) = \lambda \M(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The verification of the result above is also left to the reader.
    🔗
    Because addition and scalar multiplication have now been defined for matrices, you should not be surprised that a vector space is about to appear. First we introduce a bit of notation so that this new vector space has a name, and then we find the dimension of this new vector space.
    🔗

    Principle 3.37. \(\F{m,n}\).

    For \(m\) and \(n\) positive integers, the set of all \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrices with entries in \(\F{}\) is denoted by \(\FF{m,n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 3.38. \(\dim \F{m,n} = mn\).

    Suppose \(m\) and \(n\) are positive integers. With addition and scalar multiplication defined as above, \(\F{m,n}\) is a vector space of dimension \(mn\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    The verification that \(\F{m,n}\) is a vector space is left to the reader. Note that the additive identity of \(\F{m,n}\) is the \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix all of whose entries equal \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The reader should also verify that the list of distinct \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrices that have \(0\) in all entries except for a \(1\) in one entry is a basis of \(\FF{m,n}\text{.}\) There are \(mn\) such matrices, so the dimension of \(\F{m,n}\) equals \(mn\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.3.3 Matrix Multiplication

    Suppose, as previously, that \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) is a basis of \(V\) and \(w_1, \dots, w_m\) is a basis of \(W{}\text{.}\) Suppose also that \(u_1, \dots, u_p\) is a basis of \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Consider linear maps \(T\colon U \rightarrow V\) and \(S\colon V \rightarrow W{}\text{.}\) The composition \(ST\) is a linear map from \(U\) to \(W{}\text{.}\) Does \(\M(ST)\) equal \(\M(S) \M(T)\text{?}\) This question does not yet make sense because we have not defined the product of two matrices. We will choose a definition of matrix multiplication that forces this question to have a positive answer. Let’s see how to do this.
    🔗
    Suppose \(\M(S) = A\) and \(\M(T) = B\text{.}\) For \(1 \le k \le p\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} (ST)u_k \amp = S( \sum_{r=1}^n B_{r,k} v_r )\\ \amp = \sum_{r=1}^n B_{r,k} Sv_r\\ \amp = \sum_{r=1}^n B_{r,k} \sum_{j=1}^m A_{j,r} w_j\\ \amp = \sum_{j=1}^m ( \sum_{r=1}^n A_{j,r} B_{r,k} ) w_j\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus \(\M(ST)\) is the \(m\)-by-\(p\) matrix whose entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) equals
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{r=1}^n A_{j,r} B_{r,k}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now we see how to define matrix multiplication so that the desired equation \(\M(ST) = \M(S) \M(T)\) holds.
    🔗

    Definition 3.39. matrix multiplication.

    Suppose \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix and \(B\) is an \(n\)-by-\(p\) matrix. Then \(AB\) is defined to be the \(m\)-by-\(p\) matrix whose entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) is given by the equation
    \begin{equation*} (AB)_{j,k} = \sum_{r=1}^n A_{j,r} B_{r,k}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus the entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) of \(AB\) is computed by taking row \(j\) of \(A\) and column \(k\) of \(B\text{,}\) multiplying together corresponding entries, and then summing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    You may have learned this definition of matrix multiplication in an earlier course, although you may not have seen this motivation for it.
    🔗
    Note that we define the product of two matrices only when the number of columns of the first matrix equals the number of rows of the second matrix.
    🔗

    Example 3.40. matrix multiplication.

    Here we multiply together a \(3\)-by-\(2\) matrix and a \(2\)-by-\(4\) matrix, obtaining a \(3\)-by-\(4\) matrix:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 4 \\ 5 \amp 6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}6 \amp 5 \amp 4 \amp 3 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}10 \amp 7 \amp 4 \amp 1 \\ 26 \amp 19 \amp 12 \amp 5 \\ 42 \amp 31 \amp 20 \amp 9 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Matrix multiplication is not commutative —\(AB\) is not necessarily equal to \(BA\) even if both products are defined (see Exercise 3.3.4.10). Matrix multiplication is distributive and associative (see Exercises 3.3.4.11 and Exercise 3.3.4.12).
    🔗
    In the next result, we assume that the same basis of \(V\) is used in considering \(T \in \Lc(U, V)\) and \(S \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{,}\) the same basis of \(W\) is used in considering \(S \in \Lc(V{}, W)\) and \(ST \in \Lc(U,W)\text{,}\) and the same basis of \(U\) is used in considering \(T \in \Lc(U, V)\) and \(ST \in \Lc(U, W)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.41. matrix of product of linear maps.

    If \(T \in \Lc(U, V)\) and \(S \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{,}\) then \(\M(ST) = \M(S) \M(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The proof of the result above is the calculation that was done as motivation before the definition of matrix multiplication.
    🔗
    In the next piece of notation, note that as usual the first index refers to a row and the second index refers to a column, with a vertically centered dot used as a placeholder.
    🔗

    Principle 3.42. \(A_{j, \cdot} \text{,}\) \(A_{\cdot, k}\).

    Suppose \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix.
    1. If \(1 \le j \le m\text{,}\) then \(A_{j, \cdot}\) denotes the \(1\)-by-\(n\) matrix consisting of row \(j\) of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(1 \le k \le n\text{,}\) then \(A_{\cdot, k}\) denotes the \(m\)-by-\(1\) matrix consisting of column \(k\) of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.43. \(A_{j,\cdot}\) equals \(j^\nth\) row of \(A\) and \(A_{\cdot, k}\) equals \(k^\nth\) column of \(A\).

    The notation \(A_{2,\cdot}\) denotes the second row of \(A\) and \(A_{\cdot, 2}\) denotes the second column of \(A\text{.}\) Thus if \(A = \begin{pmatrix}8 \amp 4 \amp 5\\ 1 \amp 9 \amp 7 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} A_{2,\cdot} = \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 9 \amp 7 \end{pmatrix} \andd A_{\cdot, 2} = \begin{pmatrix}4 \\ 9 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The product of a \(1\)-by-\(n\) matrix and an \(n\)-by-\(1\) matrix is a \(1\)-by-\(1\) matrix. However, we will frequently identify a \(1\)-by-\(1\) matrix with its entry. For example,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}3 \amp 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}6 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}26 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
    because \(3 \cdot 6 + 4 \cdot 2 = 26\text{.}\) However, we can identify \(\begin{pmatrix}26 \end{pmatrix}\) with \(26\text{,}\) writing \(\begin{pmatrix}3 \amp 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}6 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 26\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The next result uses the convention discussed in the paragraph above to give another way to think of matrix multiplication. For example, the next result and the calculation in the paragraph above explain why the entry in row \(2\text{,}\) column \(1\text{,}\) of the product in Example 3.40 equals \(26\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.44. entry of matrix product equals row times column.

    Suppose \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix and \(B\) is an \(n\)-by-\(p\) matrix. Then
    \begin{equation*} (AB)_{j,k} = A_{j,\cdot} B_{\cdot, k} \end{equation*}
    if \(1 \le j \le m\) and \(1 \le k \le p\text{.}\) In other words, the entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) of \(AB\) equals (row \(j\) of \(A\)) times (column \(k\) of \(B\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(1 \le j \le m\) and \(1 \le k \le p\text{.}\) The definition of matrix multiplication states that
    \begin{equation} (AB)_{j,k} = A_{j,1} B_{1,k} + \cdots + A_{j,n} B_{n,k}.\tag{3.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The definition of matrix multiplication also implies that the product of the \(1\)-by-\(n\) matrix \(A_{j, \cdot}\) and the \(n\)-by-\(1\) matrix \(B_{\cdot, k}\) is the \(1\)-by-\(1\) matrix whose entry is the number on the right side of the equation above. Thus the entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\text{,}\) of \(AB\) equals (row \(j\) of \(A\)) times (column \(k\) of \(B\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result gives yet another way to think of matrix multiplication. In the result below, \((AB)_{\cdot,k}\) is column \(k\) of the \(m\)-by-\(p\) matrix \(AB\text{.}\) Thus \((AB)_{\cdot,k}\) is an \(m\)-by-\(1\) matrix. Also, \(A B_{\cdot, k}\) is an \(m\)-by-\(1\) matrix because it is the product of an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix and an \(n\)-by-\(1\) matrix. Thus the two sides of the equation in the result below have the same size, making it reasonable that they might be equal.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.45. column of matrix product equals matrix times column.

    Suppose \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix and \(B\) is an \(n\)-by-\(p\) matrix. Then
    \begin{equation*} (AB)_{\cdot,k} = A B_{\cdot, k} \end{equation*}
    if \(1 \le k \le p\text{.}\) In other words, column \(k\) of \(AB\) equals \(A\) times column \(k\) of \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    As discussed above, \((AB)_{\cdot,k}\) and \(A B_{\cdot, k}\) are both \(m\)-by-\(1\) matrices. If \(1 \le j \le m\text{,}\) then the entry in row \(j\) of \((AB)_{\cdot,k}\) is the left side of (3.3) and the entry in row \(j\) of \(A B_{\cdot, k}\) is the right side of (3.3). Thus \((AB)_{\cdot,k} = A B_{\cdot, k}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Our next result will give another way of thinking about the product of an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix and an \(n\)-by-\(1\) matrix, motivated by the next example.
    🔗

    Example 3.46. product of a 3-by-2 matrix and a 2-by-1 matrix.

    Use our definitions and basic arithmetic to verify that
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}1 \amp 2 \\ 3 \amp 4 \\ 5 \amp 6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}7 \\ 19 \\ 31 \end{pmatrix} = 5 \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} + 1 \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 4 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus in this example, the product of a \(3\)-by-\(2\) matrix and a \(2\)-by-\(1\) matrix is a linear combination of the columns of the \(3\)-by-\(2\) matrix, with the scalars (\(5\) and \(1\)) that multiply the columns coming from the \(2\)-by-\(1\) matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result generalizes the example above.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.47. linear combination of columns.

    Suppose \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix and \(b = \begin{pmatrix}b_1\\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{pmatrix}\) is an \(n\)-by-\(1\) matrix. Then
    \begin{equation*} Ab = b_1 A_{\cdot, 1} + \dots + b_n A_{\cdot, n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In other words, \(Ab\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(A\text{,}\) with the scalars that multiply the columns coming from \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    If \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\text{,}\) then the definition of matrix multiplication implies that the entry in row \(k\) of the \(m\)-by-\(1\) matrix \(Ab\) is
    \begin{equation*} A_{k, 1} b_1 + \cdots + A_{k,n} b_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The entry in row \(k\) of \(b_1 A_{\cdot, 1} + \dots + b_n A_{\cdot, n}\) also equals the number displayed above.
    🔗
    Because \(Ab\) and \(b_1 A_{\cdot, 1} + \dots + b_n A_{\cdot, n}\) have the same entry in row \(k\) for each \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\text{,}\) we conclude that \(Ab = b_1 A_{\cdot, 1} + \dots + b_n A_{\cdot, n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Our two previous results focus on the columns of a matrix. Analogous results hold for the rows of a matrix. Specifically, see Exercises 3.3.4.8 and Exercises 3.3.4, which can be proved using appropriate modifications of the proofs of Theorem 3.45 and Theorem 3.47.
    🔗
    The next result is the main tool used in the next subsection to prove the column–row factorization Theorem 3.53 and to prove that the column rank of a matrix equals the row rank Theorem 3.54. To be consistent with the notation often used with the column–row factorization, including in the next subsection, the matrices in the next result are called \(C\) and \(R\) instead of \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.48. matrix multiplication as linear combinations of columns or rows.

    Suppose \(C\) is an \(m\)-by-\(c\) matrix and \(R\) is a \(c\)-by-\(n\) matrix.
    1. If \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, n \}\text{,}\) then column \(k\) of \(CR\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(C\text{,}\) with the coefficients of this linear combination coming from column \(k\) of \(R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(j \in \{ 1, \ldots, m \}\text{,}\) then row \(j\) of \(CR\) is a linear combination of the rows of \(R\text{,}\) with the coefficients of this linear combination coming from row \(j\) of \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, n \}\text{.}\) Then column \(k\) of \(CR\) equals \(CR_{\cdot, k}\) (by Theorem 3.45), which equals the linear combination of the columns of \(C\) with coefficients coming from \(R_{\cdot, k}\) (by Theorem 3.47). Thus (a) holds.
    🔗
    To prove (b), follow the pattern of the proof of (a) but use rows instead of columns and use Exercises 3.3.4.8 and Exercises 3.3.4 instead of Theorem 3.45 and Theorem 3.47.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.3.4 Column–Row Factorization and Rank of a Matrix

    We begin by defining two nonnegative integers associated with each matrix.
    🔗

    Definition 3.49. column rank, row rank.

    Suppose \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix with entries in \(\F{}{}\text{.}\)
    1. The column rank of \(A\) is the dimension of the span of the columns of \(A\) in \(\FF{m,1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The row rank of \(A\) is the dimension of the span of the rows of \(A\) in \(\FF{1, n}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix, then the column rank of \(A\) is at most \(n\) (because \(A\) has \(n\) columns) and the column rank of \(A\) is also at most \(m\) (because \(\dim \F{m,1} = m\)). Similarly, the row rank of \(A\) is also at most \(\min \{ m,n \}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 3.50. column rank and row rank of a 2-by-4 matrix.

    Suppose
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{pmatrix}4 \amp 7 \amp 1 \amp 8 \\ 3 \amp 5 \amp 2 \amp 9 \end{pmatrix} \text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The column rank of \(A\) is the dimension of
    \begin{equation*} \Span \left( \begin{pmatrix}4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} , \begin{pmatrix}7 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} , \begin{pmatrix}1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} , \begin{pmatrix}8 \\9 \end{pmatrix} \right) \end{equation*}
    in \(\FF{2,1}\text{.}\) Neither of the first two vectors listed above in \(\F{2,1}\) is a scalar multiple of the other. Thus the span of this list of length four has dimension at least two. The span of this list of vectors in \(\F{2,1}\) cannot have dimension larger than two because \(\dim \FF{2,1} = 2\text{.}\) Thus the span of this list has dimension two, which means that the column rank of \(A\) is two.
    🔗
    The row rank of \(A\) is the dimension of
    \begin{equation*} \Span \Bigl( \begin{pmatrix}4 \amp 7 \amp 1 \amp 8 \end{pmatrix} , \begin{pmatrix}3 \amp 5 \amp 2 \amp 9 \end{pmatrix} \Bigr) \end{equation*}
    in \(\FF{1, 4}\text{.}\) Neither of the two vectors listed above in \(\F{1, 4}\) is a scalar multiple of the other. Thus the span of this list of length two has dimension two, which means that the row rank of \(A\) is two.
    🔗
    🔗
    We now define the transpose of a matrix.
    🔗

    Definition 3.51. transpose, \(\tran{A}\).

    The transpose of a matrix \(A\text{,}\) denoted by \(\tran{A}{}\text{,}\) is the matrix obtained from \(A\) by interchanging rows and columns. Specifically, if \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix, then \(\tran{A}\) is the \(n\)-by-\(m\) matrix whose entries are given by the equation
    \begin{equation*} ( \tran{A} )_{k,j} = A_{j, k}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.52. transpose of a matrix.

    If \(A = \begin{pmatrix}5 \amp -7\\ 3 \amp 8 \\ -4 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{,}\) then \(\tran{A} = \begin{pmatrix}5 \amp 3 \amp -4 \\ -7 \amp 8 \amp 2 \end{pmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that here \(A\) is a \(3\)-by-\(2\) matrix and \(\tran{A}\) is a \(2\)-by-\(3\) matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    The transpose has nice algebraic properties: \(\tran{(A+B)} = \tran{A} + \tran{B}{}\text{,}\) \(\tran{(\lambda A)} = \lambda \tran{A}{}\text{,}\) and \(\tran{(AC)} = \tran{C} \tran{A}\) for all \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrices \(A, B\text{,}\) all \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) and all \(n\)-by-\(p\) matrices \(C\) (see Exercises 3.3.4.14 and Exercise 3.3.4.15).
    🔗
    The next result will be the main tool used to prove that the column rank equals the row rank (see 3.54).
    🔗

    Theorem 3.53. column–row factorization.

    Suppose \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(n\) matrix with entries in \(\F{}\) and column rank \(c \ge 1\text{.}\) Then there exist an \(m\)-by-\(c\) matrix \(C\) and a \(c\)-by-\(n\) matrix \(R\text{,}\) both with entries in \(\F{}{}\text{,}\) such that \(A = CR\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Each column of \(A\) is an \(m\)-by-\(1\) matrix. The list \(A_{\cdot, 1}, \ldots, A_{\cdot, n}\) of columns of \(A\) can be reduced to a basis of the span of the columns of \(A\) (by Theorem 2.29). This basis has length \(c\text{,}\) by the definition of the column rank. The \(c\) columns in this basis can be put together to form an \(m\)-by-\(c\) matrix \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, n \}\text{,}\) then column \(k\) of \(A\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(C\text{.}\) Make the coefficients of this linear combination into column \(k\) of a \(c\)-by-\(n\) matrix that we call \(R\text{.}\) Then \(A = CR\text{,}\) as follows from Theorem 3.48(a).
    🔗
    🔗
    In Example 3.50, the column rank and row rank turned out to equal each other. The next result states that this happens for all matrices.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.54. column rank equals row rank.

    Suppose \(A \in \FF{m, n}\text{.}\) Then the column rank of \(A\) equals the row rank of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(c\) denote the column rank of \(A\text{.}\) If \(c = 0\text{,}\) then \(A = 0\) and hence the row rank of \(A\) also equals \(0\text{.}\) Thus we can assume that \(c \ge 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(A = CR\) be the column–row factorization of \(A\) given by Theorem 3.53, where \(C\) is an \(m\)-by-\(c\) matrix and \(R\) is a \(c\)-by-\(n\) matrix. Then 3.48(b) tells us that every row of \(A\) is a linear combination of the rows of \(R\text{.}\) Because \(R\) has \(c\) rows, this implies that the row rank of \(A\) is less than or equal to the column rank \(c\) of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To prove the inequality in the other direction, apply the previous paragraph result to \(\tran{A}{}\text{,}\) getting
    \begin{align*} \text{ column rank of } A \amp = \text{ row rank of } \tran{A}\\ \amp \le \text{ column rank of } \tran{A}\\ \amp = \text{ row rank of } A\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus the column rank of \(A\) equals the row rank of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Because the column rank equals the row rank, the last result allows us to dispense with the terms “column rank” and “row rank” and just use the simpler term “rank”.
    🔗

    Definition 3.55. rank.

    The rank of a matrix \(A \in \F{m,n}\) is the column rank of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    See 3.124 and Exercise 7.1.3.8 in Section 7.1 for alternative proofs that the column rank equals the row rank.
