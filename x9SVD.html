    Section 7.6 Consequences of Singular Value Decomposition

    Subsection 7.6.1 Norms of Linear Maps

    The singular value decomposition leads to the following upper bound for \(\lVert Tv \rVert\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 7.82. upper bound for \(\lVert Tv \rVert\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Let \(s_1\) be the largest singular value of \(T{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \lVert Tv \rVert \le s_1 \lVert v \rVert \end{equation*}
    for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    For a lower bound on \(\lVert Tv \rVert\text{,}\) look at Exercise 7.5.2.14 in Section 7.5.
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(s_1, \ldots, s_m\) denote the positive singular values of \(T\text{,}\) and let \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) be an orthonormal list in \(V\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_m\) be an orthonormal list in \(W\) that provide a singular value decomposition of \(T{}\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation} Tv = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_m \langle v, e_m \rangle f_m\tag{7.83} \end{equation}
    for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Hence if \(v \in V\) then
    \begin{align*} \lVert Tv \rVert ^2 \amp = {s_1}^{2} \lvert \langle v,e_1 \rangle \rvert ^2 + \cdots + {s_m}^{2} \lvert \langle v,e_m \rangle \rvert ^2\\ \amp \le {s_1}^{2} ( \lvert \langle v,e_1 \rangle \rvert ^2 + \cdots + \lvert \langle v,e_m \rangle \rvert ^2 )\\ \amp \le {s_1}^{2} \lVert v \rVert ^2{}\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the last inequality follows from Bessel’s inequality Theorem 6.26. Taking square roots of both sides of the inequality above shows that \(\lVert Tv \rVert \le s_1 \lVert v \rVert\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\) and \(s_1\) is the largest singular value of \(T{}\text{.}\) The result above shows that
    \begin{equation} \lVert Tv \rVert \le s_1 \text{ for all } v \in V \text{ with } \lVert v \rVert \le 1.\tag{7.84} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Taking \(v = e_1\) in (7.83) shows that \(Te_1 = s_1 f_1\text{.}\) Because \(\lVert f_1 \rVert = 1\text{,}\) this implies that \(\lVert Te_1 \rVert = s_1\text{.}\) Thus because \(\lVert e_1 \rVert = 1\text{,}\) the inequality in (7.84) leads to the equation
    \begin{equation} \max \{ \lVert Tv \rVert : v \in V \text{ and } \lVert v \rVert \le 1 \} = s_1.\tag{7.85} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The equation above is the motivation for the following definition, which defines the norm of \(T\) to be the left side of the equation above without needing to refer to singular values or the singular value decomposition.
    🔗

    Definition 7.86. norm of a linear map, \(\lVert \cdot \rVert\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then the norm of \(T\text{,}\) denoted by \(\lVert T \rVert\text{,}\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} \lVert T \rVert = \max \{ \lVert Tv \rVert : v \in V \text{ and } \lVert v \rVert \le 1 \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In general, the maximum of an infinite set of nonnegative numbers need not exist. However, the discussion before 7.86 shows that the maximum in the definition of the norm of a linear map \(T\) from \(V\) to \(W\) does indeed exist (and equals the largest singular value of \(T\)).
    🔗
    We now have two different uses of the word norm and the notation \(\lVert \cdot \rVert\text{.}\) Our first use of this notation was in connection with an inner product on \(V{}\text{,}\) when we defined \(\lVert v \rVert = \sqrt{\langle v,v \rangle }\) for each \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Our second use of the norm notation and terminology is with the definition we just made of \(\lVert T \rVert\) for \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{.}\) The norm \(\lVert T \rVert\) for \(T \in \Lc(V{},W)\) does not usually come from taking an inner product of \(T\) with itself (see Exercise 7.6.6.21). You should be able to tell from the context and from the symbols used which meaning of the norm is intended.
    🔗
    The properties of the norm on \(\Lc(V{}, W)\) listed below look identical to properties of the norm on an inner product space (see 6.9 and Theorem 6.17). The inequality in (d) is called the triangle inequality, thus using the same terminology that we used for the norm on \(V{}\text{.}\) For the reverse triangle inequality, see Exercise 7.6.6.1.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.87. basic properties of norms of linear maps.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then
    1. \(\lVert T \rVert \ge 0\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\lVert T \rVert = 0 \iff T = 0\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\lVert \lambda T \rVert = \lvert \lambda \rvert \lVert T \rVert\) for all \(\lambda \in \F{}\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\lVert S + T \rVert \le \lVert S \rVert + \lVert T \rVert\) for all \(S \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. Because \(\lVert Tv \rVert \ge 0\) for every \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) the definition of \(\lVert T \rVert\) implies that \(\lVert T \rVert \ge 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(\lVert T \rVert = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(Tv = 0\) for all \(v \in V\) with \(\lVert v \rVert \le 1\text{.}\) If \(u \in V\) with \(u \ne 0\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} Tu = \lVert u \rVert T( \frac{u}{\lVert u \rVert } ) = 0, \end{equation*}
      where the last equality holds because \(u/\lVert u \rVert\) has norm \(1\text{.}\) Because \(Tu = 0\) for all \(u \in V{}\text{,}\) we have \(T = 0\text{.}\) Conversely, if \(T = 0\) then \(Tv = 0\) for all \(v \in V\) and hence \(\lVert T \rVert = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Suppose \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{align*} \lVert \lambda T \rVert \amp = \max \{ \lVert \lambda Tv \rVert : v \in V \text{ and } \lVert v \rVert \le 1 \}\\ \amp = \lvert \lambda \rvert \max \{ \lVert Tv \rVert : v \in V \text{ and } \lVert v \rVert \le 1 \}\\ \amp = \lvert \lambda \rvert \lVert T \rVert \text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Suppose \(S \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{.}\) The definition of \(\lVert S+T \rVert\) implies that there exists \(v \in V\) such that \(\lVert v \rVert \le 1\) and \(\lVert S + T \rVert = \lVert (S+T)v \rVert\text{.}\) Now
      \begin{equation*} \lVert S+T \rVert = \lVert (S+T)v \rVert = \lVert Sv + Tv \rVert \le \lVert Sv \rVert + \lVert Tv \rVert \le \lVert S \rVert + \lVert T \rVert , \end{equation*}
      completing the proof of (d).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    For \(S, T \in \Lc(V{},W)\text{,}\) the quantity \(\lVert S-T \rVert\) is often called the distance between \(S\) and \(T{}\text{.}\) Informally, think of the condition that \(\lVert S-T \rVert\) is a small number as meaning that \(S\) and \(T\) are close together. For example, Exercise 7.6.6.9 asserts that for every \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) there is an invertible operator as close to \(T\) as we wish.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.88. alternative formulas for \(\lVert T \rVert\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then
    1. \(\lVert T \rVert =\) the largest singular value of \(T\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\lVert T \rVert = \max \{ \lVert Tv \rVert : v \in V \text{ and } \lVert v \rVert = 1 \}\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\lVert T \rVert =\) the smallest number \(c\) such that \(\lVert Tv \rVert \le c \lVert v \rVert\) for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    P
    1. See (7.85).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Let \(v \in V\) be such that \(0 \lt \lVert v \rVert \le 1\text{.}\) Let \(u = v/\lVert v \rVert\text{.}\) Then
      \begin{equation*} \lVert u \rVert = \lVert \frac{v}{\lVert v \rVert } \rVert = 1 \andd \lVert Tu \rVert =\lVert T( \frac{v}{\lVert v \rVert } ) \rVert = \frac{\lVert Tv \rVert }{\lVert v \rVert } \ge \lVert Tv \rVert . \end{equation*}
      Thus when finding the maximum of \(\lVert Tv \rVert\) with \(\lVert v \rVert \le 1\text{,}\) we can restrict attention to vectors in \(V\) with norm \(1\text{,}\) proving (b).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Suppose \(v \in V\) and \(v \ne 0\text{.}\) Then the definition of \(\lVert T \rVert\) implies that
      \begin{equation*} \lVert T( \frac{v}{\lVert v \rVert } ) \rVert \le \lVert T \rVert , \end{equation*}
      which implies that
      \begin{equation} \lVert Tv \rVert \le \lVert T \rVert \lVert v \rVert .\tag{7.89} \end{equation}
      Now suppose \(c \ge 0\) and \(\lVert Tv \rVert \le c \lVert v \rVert\) for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) This implies that
      \begin{equation*} \lVert Tv \rVert \le c \end{equation*}
      for all \(v\in V\) with \(\lVert v \rVert \le 1\text{.}\) Taking the maximum of the left side of the inequality above over all \(v \in V\) with \(\lVert v \rVert \le 1\) shows that \(\lVert T \rVert \le c\text{.}\) Thus \(\lVert T \rVert\) is the smallest number \(c\) such that \(\lVert Tv \rVert \le c \lVert v \rVert\) for all \(v \in V{}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    When working with norms of linear maps, you will probably frequently use the inequality (7.89).
    🔗
    For computing an approximation of the norm of a linear map \(T\) given the matrix of \(T\) with respect to some orthonormal bases, Theorem 7.88(a) is likely to be most useful. The matrix of \(T^*T\) is quickly computable from matrix multiplication. Then a computer can be asked to find an approximation for the largest eigenvalue of \(T^*T\) (excellent numeric algorithms exist for this purpose). Then taking the square root and using 7.88(a) gives an approximation for the norm of \(T\) (which usually cannot be computed exactly).
    🔗
    You should verify all assertions in the example below.
    🔗

    Example 7.90. norms.

    1. If \(I\) denotes the usual identity operator on \(V{}\text{,}\) then \(\lVert I \rVert = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(T \in \Lc( \F n )\) and the matrix of \(T\) with respect to the standard basis of \(\F n\) consists of all \(1\)’s, then \(\lVert T \rVert = n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(V\) has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\) with corresponding eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\text{,}\) then \(\lVert T \rVert\) is the maximum of the numbers \(\lvert \lambda_1 \rvert , \ldots, \lvert \lambda_n \rvert\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Suppose \(T\in \Lc( \R5 )\) is the operator whose matrix (with respect to the standard basis) is the \(5\)-by-\(5\) matrix whose entry in row \(j\text{,}\) column \(k\) is \(1/(j^2 + k)\text{.}\) Standard mathematical software shows that the largest singular value of \(T\) is approximately \(0.8\) and the smallest singular value of \(T\) is approximately \(10^{-6}{}\text{.}\) Thus \(\lVert T \rVert \approx 0.8\) and (using Exercise 7.5.2.10 in Section 7.5) \(\lVert T^{-1} \rVert \approx 10^6{}\text{.}\) It is not possible to find exact formulas for these norms.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    A linear map and its adjoint have the same norm, as shown by the next result.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.91. norm of the adjoint.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\text{.}\) Then \(\lVert T^* \rVert = \lVert T \rVert\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(w \in W{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \lVert T^*w \rVert ^2 = \langle T^*w, T^*w \rangle = \langle TT^*w, w \rangle \le \lVert TT^*w \rVert \lVert w \rVert \le \lVert T \rVert \lVert T^*w \rVert \lVert w \rVert . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The inequality above implies that
    \begin{equation*} \lVert T^*w \rVert \le \lVert T \rVert \lVert w \rVert , \end{equation*}
    which along with 7.88(c) implies that \(\lVert T^* \rVert \le \lVert T \rVert\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Replacing \(T\) with \(T^*\) in the inequality \(\lVert T^* \rVert \le \lVert T \rVert\) and then using the equation \(( T^* )^* = T\) shows that \(\lVert T \rVert \le \lVert T^* \rVert\text{.}\) Thus \(\lVert T^* \rVert = \lVert T \rVert\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    You may want to construct an alternative proof of the result above using Exercise 7.5.2.9 in Section 7.5, which asserts that a linear map and its adjoint have the same positive singular values.

    Subsection 7.6.2 Approximation by Linear Maps with Lower-Dimensional Range

    The next result is a spectacular application of the singular value decomposition. It says that to best approximate a linear map by a linear map whose range has dimension at most \(k\text{,}\) chop off the singular value decomposition after the first \(k\) terms. Specifically, the linear map \(T{}_k\) in the next result has the property that \(\dim \ran T{}_k = k\) and \(T{}_k\) minimizes the distance to \(T\) among all linear maps with range of dimension at most \(k\text{.}\) This result leads to algorithms for compressing huge matrices while preserving their most important information.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.92. best approximation by linear map whose range has dimension \(\le k\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V{}, W)\) and \(s_1 \ge \cdots \ge s_m\) are the positive singular values of \(T{}\text{.}\) Suppose \(1 \le k \lt m\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \min \{ \lVert T-S \rVert : S \in \Lc(V{}, W) \text{ and } \dim \ran S \le k \} = s_{k+1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Furthermore, if
    \begin{equation*} Tv = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_m \langle v, e_m \rangle f_m \end{equation*}
    is a singular value decomposition of \(T\) and \(T{}_k \in \Lc(V{},W)\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} T{}_k v = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_k \langle v, e_k \rangle f_k \end{equation*}
    for each \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then \(\dim \ran T{}_k = k\) and \(\lVert T-T{}_k \rVert = s_{k+1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    If \(v \in V\) then
    \begin{align*} \lVert (T-T{}_k)v \rVert ^2 \amp = \lVert s_{k+1} \langle v, e_{k+1} \rangle f_{k+1} + \cdots + s_m \langle v, e_m \rangle f_m \rVert ^2\\ \amp = {s_{k+1}}^{2} \lvert \langle v, e_{k+1} \rangle \rvert ^2 + \cdots +{s_m}^{2} \lvert \langle v, e_m \rangle \rvert ^2\\ \amp \le {s_{k+1}}^{2} ( \lvert \langle v, e_{k+1} \rangle \rvert ^2 + \cdots + \lvert \langle v, e_m \rangle \rvert ^2 )\\ \amp \le {s_{k+1}}^{2} \lVert v \rVert ^2{}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus \(\lVert T - T{}_k \rVert \le s_{k+1}\text{.}\) The equation \((T - T{}_k)e_{k+1} = s_{k+1} f_{k+1}\) now shows that \(\lVert T - T{}_k \rVert = s_{k+1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose \(S \in \Lc(V{},W)\) and \(\dim \ran S \le k\text{.}\) Thus \(Se_1, \ldots, Se_{k+1}\text{,}\) which is a list of length \(k+1\text{,}\) is linearly dependent. Hence there exist \(a_1, \ldots, a_{k+1} \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) not all \(0\text{,}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} a_1 Se_1 + \cdots + a_{k+1} Se_{k+1} = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now \(a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_{k+1} e_{k+1} \ne 0\) because \(a_1, \ldots, a_{k+1}\) are not all \(0\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{align*} \lVert (T - S)(a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_{k+1} e_{k+1}) \rVert ^2 \amp = \lVert T(a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_{k+1} e_{k+1}) \rVert ^2\\ \amp = \lVert s_1 a_1 f_1 + \cdots + s_{k+1} a_{k+1} f_{k+1} \rVert ^2\\ \amp = {s_1}^{2} \lvert a_1 \rvert ^2 + \cdots + {s_{k+1}}^{2} \lvert a_{k+1} \rvert ^2\\ \amp \ge {s_{k+1}}^{2} ( \lvert a_1 \rvert ^2 + \cdots + \lvert a_{k+1} \rvert ^2 )\\ \amp = {s_{k+1}}^{2} \lVert a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_{k+1} e_{k+1} \rVert ^2{}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Because \(a_1 e_1 + \cdots + a_{k+1} e_{k+1} \ne 0\text{,}\) the inequality above implies that
    \begin{equation*} \lVert T-S \rVert \ge s_{k+1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(S = T{}_k\) minimizes \(\lVert T-S \rVert\) among \(S\in\Lc(V{},W)\) with \(\dim \ran S \le k\text{.}\)

    Subsection 7.6.3 Polar Decomposition

    Recall our discussion before 7.54 of the analogy between complex numbers \(z\) with \(\lvert z \rvert = 1\) and unitary operators. Continuing with this analogy, note that every complex number \(z\) except \(0\) can be written in the form
    \begin{align*} z \amp = ( \frac{z}{|z|} ) |z|\\ \amp = ( \frac{z}{|z|} ) \sqrt{\bar{z} z}\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the first factor, namely, \(z/|z|\text{,}\) has absolute value \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Our analogy leads us to guess that every operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\) can be written as a unitary operator times \(\sqrt{T^* T}\text{.}\) That guess is indeed correct. The corresponding result is called the polar decomposition, which gives a beautiful description of an arbitrary operator on \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that if \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) then \(T^* T\) is a positive operator [as was shown in Theorem 7.64(a)] . Thus the operator \(\sqrt{T^*T}\) makes sense and is well defined as a positive operator on \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The polar decomposition that we are about to state and prove says that every operator on \(V\) is the product of a unitary operator and a positive operator. Thus we can write an arbitrary operator on \(V\) as the product of two nice operators, each of which comes from a class that we can completely describe and that we understand reasonably well. The unitary operators are described by Theorem 7.55 if \(\F{} = \C{}\text{;}\) the positive operators are described by the real and complex spectral theorems ( Theorem 7.29 and Theorem 7.31).
    🔗
    Specifically, consider the case \(\F{} = \C{}\text{,}\) and suppose
    \begin{equation*} T = S \sqrt{T^* T} \end{equation*}
    is a polar decomposition of an operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) where \(S\) is a unitary operator. Then there is an orthonormal basis of \(V\) with respect to which \(S\) has a diagonal matrix, and there is an orthonormal basis of \(V\) with respect to which \(\sqrt{T^* T}\) has a diagonal matrix. Warning: There may not exist an orthonormal basis that simultaneously puts the matrices of both \(S\) and \(\sqrt{T^* T}\) into these nice diagonal forms—\(S\) may require one orthonormal basis and \(\sqrt{T^* T}\) may require a different orthonormal basis.
    🔗
    However (still assuming that \(\F{} = \C{}\)), if \(T\) is normal, then an orthonormal basis of \(V\) can be chosen such that both \(S\) and \(\sqrt{T^*T}\) have diagonal matrices with respect to this basis—see Exercise 7.6.6.31. The converse is also true: If \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(T = S \sqrt{T^*T}\) for some unitary operator \(S \in \Lc(V)\) such that \(S\) and \(\sqrt{T^*T}\) both have diagonal matrices with respect to the same orthonormal basis of \(V{}\text{,}\) then \(T\) is normal. This holds because \(T\) then has a diagonal matrix with respect to this same orthonormal basis, which implies that \(T\) is normal [by the equivalence of (c) and (a) in Theorem 7.31] .
    🔗
    The polar decomposition below is valid on both real and complex inner product spaces and for all operators on those spaces.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.93. polar decomposition.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then there exists a unitary operator \(S \in \Lc(V)\) such that
    \begin{equation*} T = S\sqrt{T^*T}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(s_1, \ldots, s_m\) be the positive singular values of \(T\text{,}\) and let \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) and \(f_1, \dots, f_m\) be orthonormal lists in \(V\) such that
    \begin{equation} Tv = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_m \langle v, e_m \rangle f_m\tag{7.94} \end{equation}
    for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Extend \(e_1, \ldots, e_m\) and \(f_1, \dots, f_m\) to orthonormal bases \(e_1, \ldots, e_n\) and \(f_1, \dots, f_n\) of \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Define \(S \in \Lc(V)\) by
    \begin{equation*} Sv = \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + \langle v, e_n \rangle f_n \end{equation*}
    for each \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \lVert Sv \rVert ^2 \amp = \lVert \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + \langle v, e_n \rangle f_n \rVert ^2\\ \amp = \lvert \langle v, e_1 \rangle \rvert ^2 + \cdots+ \lvert \langle v, e_n \rangle \rvert ^2\\ \amp = \lVert v \rVert ^2{}\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus \(S\) is a unitary operator.
    🔗
    Applying \(T^*\) to both sides of (7.94) and then using the formula for \(T^*\) given by (7.77) shows that
    \begin{equation*} T^*Tv = {s_1}^{2} \langle v,e_1 \rangle e_1 + \cdots + {s_m}^{2} \langle v, e_m \rangle e_m \end{equation*}
    for every \(v \in V{}\text{.}\) Thus if \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{T^*T} v = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle e_1 + \cdots + s_m \langle v, e_m \rangle e_m \end{equation*}
    because the operator that sends \(v\) to the right side of the equation above is a positive operator whose square equals \(T^*T{}\text{.}\) Now
    \begin{align*} S \sqrt{T^*T} v \amp = S ( s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle e_1 + \cdots + s_m \langle v, e_m \rangle e_m )\\ \amp = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_m \langle v, e_m \rangle f_m\\ \amp = Tv\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the last equation follows from (7.94).
    🔗
    🔗
    Exercise 7.6.6.27 shows that the unitary operator \(S\) produced in the proof above is as close as a unitary operator can be to \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Alternative proofs of the polar decomposition directly use the spectral theorem, avoiding the singular value decomposition. However, the proof above seems cleaner than those alternative proofs.

    Definition 7.96. ellipsoid, \(E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n)\text{,}\) principal axes.

    Suppose that \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V\) and \(s_1, \ldots, s_n\) are positive numbers. The ellipsoid \(E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n)\) with principal axes \(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n) = \{ v \in V:\frac{\lvert \langle v,f_1 \rangle \rvert ^2}{{s_1}^{2}} + \cdots + \frac{\lvert \langle v,f_n \rangle \rvert ^2}{{s_n}^{2}} \lt 1 \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    The next result states that every invertible operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\) maps the ball \(B\) in \(V\) onto an ellipsoid in \(V{}\text{.}\) The proof shows that the principal axes of this ellipsoid come from the singular value decomposition of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 7.99. invertible operator takes ball to ellipsoid.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is invertible. Then \(T\) maps the ball \(B\) in \(V\) onto an ellipsoid in \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(T\) has singular value decomposition
    \begin{equation} Tv = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_n \langle v, e_n \rangle f_n\tag{7.100} \end{equation}
    for all \(v \in V\text{;}\) here \(s_1, \ldots, s_n\) are the singular values of \(T\) and \(e_1, \ldots, e_n\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) are both orthonormal bases of \(V{}\text{.}\) We will show that \(T(B) = E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First suppose \(v \in B\text{.}\) Because \(T\) is invertible, none of the singular values \(s_1, \ldots, s_n\) equals \(0\) (see 7.68). Thus (7.100) implies that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\lvert \langle Tv,f_1 \rangle \rvert ^2}{{s_1}^{2}} + \cdots + \frac{\lvert \langle Tv,f_n \rangle \rvert ^2}{{s_n}^{2}} = \lvert \langle v, e_1 \rangle \rvert ^2 + \cdots + \lvert \langle v, e_n \rangle \rvert ^2 \lt 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(Tv \in E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n)\text{.}\) Hence \(T(B) \subset E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To prove inclusion in the other direction, now suppose \(w \in E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n)\text{.}\) Let
    \begin{equation*} v = \frac{\langle w, f_1 \rangle }{s_1} e_1 + \cdots + \frac{\langle w, f_n \rangle }{s_n} e_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then \(\lVert v \rVert \lt 1\) and (7.100) implies that \(Tv = \langle w,f_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + \langle w, f_n \rangle f_n = w\text{.}\) Thus \(T(B) \supset E(s_1 f_1, \ldots, s_n f_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We now use the previous result to show that invertible operators take all ellipsoids, not just the ball of radius \(1\text{,}\) to ellipsoids.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.101. invertible operator takes ellipsoids to ellipsoids.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is invertible and \(E\) is an ellipsoid in \(V{}\text{.}\) Then \(T(E)\) is an ellipsoid in \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    There exist an orthonormal basis \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) of \(V\) and positive numbers \(s_1, \ldots, s_n\) such that \(E = E(s_1f_1, \ldots, s_nf_n)\text{.}\) Define \(S \in \Lc(V)\) by
    \begin{equation*} S( a_1 f_1 + \cdots + a_n f_n) = a_1 s_1 f_1 + \cdots + a_n s_n f_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then \(S\) maps the ball \(B\) of \(V\) onto \(E\text{,}\) as you can verify. Thus
    \begin{equation*} T(E) = T( S(B) ) = (TS)(B). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The equation above and Theorem 7.99, applied to \(TS\text{,}\) show that \(T(E)\) is an ellipsoid in \(V{}\text{.}\)

    Subsection 7.6.5 Volume via Singular Values

    Our goal in this subsection is to understand how an operator changes the volume of subsets of its domain. Because notions of volume belong to analysis rather than to linear algebra, we will work only with an intuitive notion of volume. Our intuitive approach to volume can be converted into appropriate correct definitions, correct statements, and correct proofs using the machinery of analysis.
    🔗
    Our intuition about volume works best in real inner product spaces. Thus the assumption that \(\F{} = \R{}\) will appear frequently in the rest of this subsection.
    🔗
    If \(\dim V = n\text{,}\) then by volume we will mean \(n\)-dimensional volume. You should be familiar with this concept in \(\R3\text{.}\) When \(n=2\text{,}\) this is usually called area instead of volume, but for consistency we use the word volume in all dimensions. The most fundamental intuition about volume is that the volume of a box (whose defining edges are by definition orthogonal to each other) is the product of the lengths of the defining edges. Thus we make the following definition.
    🔗

    Definition 7.108. volume of a box.

    Suppose \(\F{} = \R{}\text{.}\) If \(u \in V\) and \(r_1, \ldots, r_n\) are positive numbers and \(e_1, \ldots, e_n\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V{}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \vol ( u + P(r_1 e_1, \ldots, r_n e_n) ) = r_1 \times \cdots \times r_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The definition above agrees with the familiar formulas for the area (which we are calling the volume) of a rectangle in \(\R2\) and for the volume of a box in \(\R3\text{.}\) For example, the first box in Example 7.106 has two-dimensional volume (or area) \(2\) because the defining edges of that box have length \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sqrt{2}\text{.}\) The second box in Example 7.106 has three-dimensional volume \(2\) because the defining edges of that box have length \(1\text{,}\) \(2\text{,}\) and \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    Missing description
    🔗
    Volume of this ball \(\approx\) sum of the volumes of the five boxes.
    🔗
    To define the volume of a subset of \(V{}\text{,}\) approximate the subset by a finite collection of disjoint boxes, and then add up the volumes of the approximating collection of boxes. As we approximate a subset of \(V\) more accurately by disjoint unions of more boxes, we get a better approximation to the volume.
    🔗
    These ideas should remind you of how the Riemann integral is defined by approximating the area under a curve by a disjoint collection of rectangles. This discussion leads to the following nonrigorous but intuitive definition.
    🔗

    Definition 7.109. volume.

    Suppose \(\F{} = \R{}\) and \(\Omega \subset V{}\text{.}\) Then the volume of \(\Omega\text{,}\) denoted by \(\vol \Omega\text{,}\) is approximately the sum of the volumes of a collection of disjoint boxes that approximate \(\Omega\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    We are ignoring many reasonable questions by taking an intuitive approach to volume. For example, if we approximate \(\Omega\) by boxes with respect to one basis, do we get the same volume if we approximate \(\Omega\) by boxes with respect to a different basis? If \(\Omega_1\) and \(\Omega_2\) are disjoint subsets of \(V{}\text{,}\) is \(\vol(\Omega_1 \cup \Omega_2) = \vol \Omega_1 + \vol \Omega_2\text{?}\) Provided that we consider only reasonably nice subsets of \(V{}\text{,}\) techniques of analysis show that both these questions have affirmative answers that agree with our intuition about volume.
    🔗

    Example 7.110. volume change by a linear map.

    
    Missing description
    🔗
    Each box here has twice the width and the same height as the boxes in the previous figure.
    🔗
    Suppose that \(T \in \Lc( \R2 )\) is defined by \(Tv = 2 \langle v, e_1 \rangle e_1 + \langle v, e_2 \rangle e_2\text{,}\) where \(e_1, e_2\) is the standard basis of \(\R2\text{.}\) This linear map stretches vectors along the \(e_1\)-axis by a factor of \(2\) and leaves vectors along the \(e_2\)-axis unchanged. The ball approximated by five boxes above gets mapped by \(T\) to the ellipsoid shown here. Each of the five boxes in the original figure gets mapped to a box of twice the width and the same height as in the original figure. Hence each box gets mapped to a box of twice the volume (area) as in the original figure. The sum of the volumes of the five new boxes approximates the volume of the ellipsoid. Thus \(T\) changes the volume of the ball by a factor of \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In the example above, \(T\) maps boxes with respect to the basis \(e_1, e_2\) to boxes with respect to the same basis; thus we can see how \(T\) changes volume. In general, an operator maps boxes to parallelepipeds that are not boxes. However, if we choose the right basis (coming from the singular value decomposition!), then boxes with respect to that basis get mapped to boxes with respect to a possibly different basis, as shown in Theorem 7.107. This observation leads to a natural proof of the following result.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.111. volume changes by a factor of the product of the singular values.

    Suppose \(\F{} = \R{}\text{,}\) \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is invertible, and \(\Omega \subset V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \vol T(\Omega) = (\text{ product of singular values of } T)(\vol \Omega). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(T\) has singular value decomposition
    \begin{equation*} Tv = s_1 \langle v, e_1 \rangle f_1 + \cdots + s_n \langle v, e_n \rangle f_n \end{equation*}
    for all \(v \in V{}\text{,}\) where \(e_1, \ldots, e_n\) and \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) are orthonormal bases of \(V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Approximate \(\Omega\) by boxes of the form \(u + P(r_1 e_1, \ldots, r_n e_n)\text{,}\) which have volume \(r_1 \times \cdots \times r_n\text{.}\) The operator \(T\) maps each box \(u + P(r_1 e_1, \ldots, r_n e_n)\) onto the box \(Tu + P(r_1 s_1 f_1, \ldots, r_n s_n f_n)\text{,}\) which has volume \((s_1 \times \cdots \times s_n)(r_1 \times \cdots \times r_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The operator \(T\) maps a collection of boxes that approximate \(\Omega\) onto a collection of boxes that approximate \(T(\Omega)\text{.}\) Because \(T\) changes the volume of each box in a collection that approximates \(\Omega\) by a factor of \(s_1 \times \cdots \times s_n\text{,}\) the linear map \(T\) changes the volume of \(\Omega\) by the same factor.
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) As we will see when we get to determinants, the product of the singular values of \(T\) equals \(\lvert \det T \rvert\text{;}\) see 9.60 and Theorem 9.61.

    Subsection 7.6.6 Properties of an Operator as Determined by Its Eigenvalues

    We conclude this chapter by presenting the table below. The context of this table is a finite-dimensional complex inner product space. The first column of the table shows a property that a normal operator on such a space might have. The second column of the table shows a subset of \(\C{}\) such that the operator has the corresponding property if and only if all eigenvalues of the operator lie in the specified subset. For example, the first row of the table states that a normal operator is invertible if and only if all its eigenvalues are nonzero (this first row is the only one in the table that does not need the hypothesis that the operator is normal).
    🔗
    Make sure you can explain why all results in the table hold. For example, the last row of the table holds because the norm of an operator equals its largest singular value (by (7.85)) and the singular values of a normal operator, assuming \(\F{} = \C{}\text{,}\) equal the absolute values of the eigenvalues (by Exercise 7.5.2.7 in Section 7.5).
    🔗
    properties of a normal operator eigenvalues are contained in
    invertible \(\C{} \sm \{ 0 \}\)
    self-adjoint \(\R{}\)
    skew \(\{ \lambda \in \C{}: \re \lambda = 0 \}\)
    orthogonal projection \(\{ 0, 1 \}\)
    positive \([0, \infty)\)
    unitary \(\{ \lambda \in \C{}: \lvert \lambda \rvert = 1 \}\)
