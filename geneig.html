    Section 8.1 Generalized Eigenvectors and Nilpotent Operators

    Subsection 8.1.1 Null Spaces of Powers of an Operator

    We begin this chapter with a study of null spaces of powers of an operator.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.1. sequence of increasing null spaces.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \{0\} = \ker T^0 \subset \ker T^1 \subset \cdots \subset \ker T^k \subset \ker T^{k+1} \subset \dotsb . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(k\) is a nonnegative integer and \(v \in \ker T^k\!\text{.}\) Then \(T^k v = 0\text{,}\) which implies that \(T^{k+1} v = T( T^kv ) = T(0) = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(v \in \ker T^{k+1}\!\text{.}\) Hence \(\ker T^k \subset \ker T^{k+1}\!\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    For similar results about decreasing sequences of ranges, see Exercises 8.1.3.6, Exercise 8.1.3.7, and Exercise 8.1.3.8.
    🔗
    The following result states that if two consecutive terms in the sequence of subspaces above are equal, then all later terms in the sequence are equal.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.2. equality in the sequence of null spaces.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(m\) is a nonnegative integer such that
    \begin{equation*} \ker T^m = \ker T^{m+1}\!. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then
    \begin{equation*} \ker T^m = \ker T^{m+1} = \ker T^{m+2} = \ker T^{m+3} = \dotsb . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(k\) be a positive integer. We want to prove that
    \begin{equation*} \ker T^{m+k} = \ker T^{m + k + 1}\!. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We already know from Theorem 8.1 that \(\ker T^{m+k} \subset \ker T^{m + k + 1}\!\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To prove the inclusion in the other direction, suppose \(v \in \ker T^{m+ k+1}\!\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} T^{m+1} ( T^k v ) = T^{m+k+1}v =0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence
    \begin{equation*} T^k v \in \ker T^{m+1} = \ker T^m\!. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus \(T^{m+k} v = T^m( T^k v ) = 0\text{,}\) which means that \(v \in \ker T^{m+k}\!\text{.}\) This implies that \(\ker T^{m + k+1} \subset \ker T^{m+k}\!\text{,}\) completing the proof.
    🔗
    🔗
    The result above raises the question of whether there exists a nonnegative integer \(m\) such that \(\ker T^m = \ker T^{m+1}\!\text{.}\) The next result shows that this equality holds at least when \(m\) equals the dimension of the vector space on which \(T\) operates.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.3. null spaces stop growing.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \ker T^{\dim V} = \ker T^{\dim V + 1} = \ker T^{\dim V + 2} = \dotsb . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    We only need to prove that \(\ker T^{\dim V} = \ker T^{\dim V + 1}\) (by Theorem 8.2). Suppose this is not true. Then, by Theorem 8.1 and Theorem 8.2, we have
    \begin{equation*} \{0\} = \ker T^0 \subsetneq \ker T^1 \subsetneq \dots \subsetneq \ker T^{\dim V} \subsetneq \ker T^{\dim V + 1}\!, \end{equation*}
    where the symbol \(\subsetneq\) means “contained in but not equal to”. At each of the strict inclusions in the chain above, the dimension increases by at least \(1\text{.}\) Thus \(\dim \ker T^{\dim V + 1} \ge \dim V+ 1\text{,}\) a contradiction because a subspace of \(V\) cannot have a larger dimension than \(\dim V{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    It is not true that \(V = \ker T \oplus \ran T\) for every \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) However, the next result can be a useful substitute.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.4. \(V\) is the direct sum of \(\ker T^{\dim V}\) and \(\ran T^{\dim V}\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} V = \ker T^{\dim V} \oplus \ran T^{\dim V}\!. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(n = \dim V{}\text{.}\) First we show that
    \begin{equation} ( \ker T^n ) \cap ( \ran T^n ) = \{0\}.\tag{8.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Suppose \(v \in ( \ker T^n ) \cap ( \ran T^n )\text{.}\) Then \(T^n v = 0\text{,}\) and there exists \(u \in V\) such that \(v = T^n u\text{.}\) Applying \(T^n\) to both sides of the last equation shows that \(T^n v = T^{2n} u\text{.}\) Hence \(T^{2n} u = 0\text{,}\) which implies that \(T^n u = 0\) (by Theorem 8.3). Thus \(v = T^n u = 0\text{,}\) completing the proof of (8.5).
    🔗
    Now (8.5) implies that \(\ker T^n + \ran T^n\) is a direct sum (by Theorem 1.46). Also,
    \begin{equation*} \dim( \ker T^n \oplus \ran T^n ) = \dim \ker T^n + \dim \ran T^n = \dim V{}, \end{equation*}
    where the first equality above comes from Theorem 3.94 and the second equality comes from the fundamental theorem of linear maps Theorem 3.21. The equation above implies that \(\ker T^n \oplus \ran T^n = V{}\) (see 2.39), as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    For an improvement of the result above, see Exercise 8.1.3.19.
    🔗

    Example 8.6. \(\F3 = \ker T^3 \oplus \ran T^3\) for \(T \in \Lc(\F3)\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(\F{3})\) is defined by
    \begin{equation*} T(z_1, z_2, z_3) = (4z_2, 0, 5z_3). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then \(\ker T = \{ (z_1, 0, 0): z_1 \in \F{} \}\) and \(\ran T = \{ (z_1, 0, z_3): z_1, z_3 \in \F{} \}\text{.}\) Thus \(\ker T \cap \ran T \ne \{0\}\text{.}\) Hence \(\ker T + \ran T\) is not a direct sum. Also note that \(\ker T + \ran T \ne \FF3\text{.}\) However, we have \(T^3(z_1, z_2, z_3) = (0, 0, 125z_3)\text{.}\) Thus we see that
    \begin{equation*} \ker T^3 = \{ (z_1, z_2, 0): z_1, z_2 \in \F{} \} \andd \ran T^3 = \{ (0, 0, z_3): z_3 \in \F{} \}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence \(\FF3 = \ker T^3 \oplus \ran T^3{}\text{,}\) as expected by Theorem 8.4.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.2 Generalized Eigenvectors

    Some operators do not have enough eigenvectors to lead to good descriptions of their behavior. Thus in this subsection we introduce the concept of generalized eigenvectors, which will play a major role in our description of the structure of an operator.
    🔗
    To understand why we need more than eigenvectors, let’s examine the question of describing an operator by decomposing its domain into invariant subspaces. Fix \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) We seek to describe \(T\) by finding a “nice” direct sum decomposition
    \begin{equation*} V = V{}_1 \oplus \dots \oplus V{}_n, \end{equation*}
    where each \(V{}_k\) is a subspace of \(V\) invariant under \(T{}\text{.}\) The simplest possible nonzero invariant subspaces are one-dimensional. A decomposition as above in which each \(V{}_k\) is a one-dimensional subspace of \(V\) invariant under \(T\) is possible if and only if \(V\) has a basis consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\) (see 5.55). This happens if and only if \(V\) has an eigenspace decomposition
    \begin{equation} V = E(\lambda_1, T) \oplus \dots \oplus E(\lambda_m, T),\tag{8.7} \end{equation}
    where \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m\) are the distinct eigenvalues of \(T\) (see 5.55).
    🔗
    The spectral theorem in the previous chapter shows that if \(V\) is an inner product space, then a decomposition of the form (8.7) holds for every self-adjoint operator if \(\F{} = \R{}\) and for every normal operator if \(\F{} = \C{}\) because operators of those types have enough eigenvectors to form a basis of \(V\) (see 7.29 and Theorem 7.31).
    🔗
    However, a decomposition of the form (8.7) may not hold for more general operators, even on a complex vector space. An example was given by the operator in Example 5.57, which does not have enough eigenvectors for (8.7) to hold. Generalized eigenvectors and generalized eigenspaces, which we now introduce, will remedy this situation.
    🔗

    Definition 8.8. generalized eigenvector.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) and \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) A vector \(v \in V\) is called a generalized eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to \(\lambda\) if \(v \ne 0\) and
    \begin{equation*} (T - \lambda I)^k v = 0 \end{equation*}
    for some positive integer \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    Generalized eigenvalues are not defined because doing so would not lead to anything new. Reason: if \((T - \lambda I)^k\) is not injective for some positive integer \(k\text{,}\) then \(T - \lambda I\) is not injective, and hence \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A nonzero vector \(v \in V\) is a generalized eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to \(\lambda\) if and only if
    \begin{equation*} (T - \lambda I)^{\dim V} v = 0, \end{equation*}
    as follows from applying 8.1 and Theorem 8.3 to the operator \(T - \lambda I\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As we know, an operator on a complex vector space may not have enough eigenvectors to form a basis of the domain. The next result shows that on a complex vector space there are enough generalized eigenvectors to do this.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.9. a basis of generalized eigenvectors.

    Suppose \(\F{} = \C{}\) and \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then there is a basis of \(V\) consisting of generalized eigenvectors of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(n = \dim V{}\text{.}\) We will use induction on \(n\text{.}\) To get started, note that the desired result holds if \(n=1\) because then every nonzero vector in \(V\) is an eigenvector of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Aside
    This step is where we use the hypothesis that \(\F{} = \C{}\text{,}\) because if \(\F{} = \R{}\) then \(T\) may not have any eigenvalues.
    🔗
    Now suppose \(n > 1\) and the desired result holds for all smaller values of \(\dim V{}\text{.}\) Let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) Applying 8.4 to \(T - \lambda I\) shows that
    \begin{equation*} V = \ker(T - \lambda I)^n \oplus \ran (T - \lambda I)^n{}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If \(\ker(T - \lambda I)^n = V{}\text{,}\) then every nonzero vector in \(V\) is a generalized eigenvector of \(T\text{,}\) and thus in this case there is a basis of \(V\) consisting of generalized eigenvectors of \(T{}\text{.}\) Hence we can assume that \(\ker(T - \lambda I)^n \ne V{}\text{,}\) which implies that \(\ran (T - \lambda I)^n \ne \{ 0 \}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Also, \(\ker(T - \lambda I)^n \ne \{ 0 \}\text{,}\) because \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) Thus we have
    \begin{equation*} 0 \lt \dim \ran(T - \lambda I)^n \lt n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Furthermore, \(\ran(T - \lambda I)^n\) is invariant under \(T\) [by Theorem 5.18 with \(p(z) = (z - \lambda)^n\)] . Let \(S \in \Lc( \ran(T - \lambda I)^n )\) equal \(T\) restricted to \(\ran(T - \lambda I)^n{}\text{.}\) Our induction hypothesis applied to the operator \(S\) implies that there is a basis of \(\ran(T - \lambda I)^n\) consisting of generalized eigenvectors of \(S\text{,}\) which of course are generalized eigenvectors of \(T{}\text{.}\) Adjoining that basis of \(\ran(T - \lambda I)^n\) to a basis of \(\ker(T - \lambda I)^n\) gives a basis of \(V\) consisting of generalized eigenvectors of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(\F{} = \R{}\) and \(\dim V > 1\text{,}\) then some operators on \(V\) have the property that there exists a basis of \(V\) consisting of generalized eigenvectors of the operator, and (unlike what happens when \(\F{}=\C{}\)) other operators do not have this property. See Exercise 8.1.3.11 for a necessary and sufficient condition that determines whether an operator has this property.
    🔗

    Example 8.10. generalized eigenvectors of an operator on \(\C3\).

    Define \(T \in \Lc( \C{3} )\) by
    \begin{equation*} T(z_1, z_2, z_3) = (4z_2, 0, 5z_3) \end{equation*}
    for each \((z_1, z_2, z_3) \in \CC3\text{.}\) A routine use of the definition of eigenvalue shows that the eigenvalues of \(T\) are \(0\) and \(5\text{.}\) Furthermore, the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue \(0\) are the nonzero vectors of the form \((z_1, 0, 0)\text{,}\) and the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue \(5\) are the nonzero vectors of the form \((0, 0, z_3)\text{.}\) Hence this operator does not have enough eigenvectors to span its domain \(\CC3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We compute that \(T^3(z_1, z_2, z_3) = (0, 0, 125 z_3)\text{.}\) Thus 8.1 and Theorem 8.3 imply that the generalized eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(0\) are the nonzero vectors of the form \((z_1, z_2, 0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We also have \((T-5I)^3(z_1, z_2, z_3) = (-125z_1 + 300z_2, -125z_2, 0)\text{.}\) Thus the generalized eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(5\) are the nonzero vectors of the form \((0,0,z_3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The paragraphs above show that each of the standard basis vectors of \(\C3\) is a generalized eigenvector of \(T{}\text{.}\) Thus \(\C3\) indeed has a basis consisting of generalized eigenvectors of \(T{}\text{,}\) as promised by Theorem 8.9.
    🔗
    🔗
    If \(v\) is an eigenvector of \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{,}\) then the corresponding eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is uniquely determined by the equation \(Tv = \lambda v\text{,}\) which can be satisfied by only one \(\lambda \in \F{}\) (because \(v\ne 0\)). However, if \(v\) is a generalized eigenvector of \(T\text{,}\) then it is not obvious that the equation \((T - \lambda I)^{\dim V}v = 0\) can be satisfied by only one \(\lambda \in \F{}{}\text{.}\) Fortunately, the next result tells us that all is well on this issue.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.11. generalized eigenvector corresponds to a unique eigenvalue.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then each generalized eigenvector of \(T\) corresponds to only one eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose \(v \in V\) is a generalized eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to eigenvalues \(\alpha\) and \(\lambda\) of \(T{}\text{.}\) Let \(m\) be the smallest positive integer such that \((T - \alpha I)^m v = 0\text{.}\) Let \(n = \dim V{}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} 0 \amp = (T - \lambda I)^n v\\ \amp = ( (T - \alpha I) + (\alpha - \lambda) I )^n v\\ \amp = \sum_{k=0}^n b_k (\alpha - \lambda)^{n-k} (T - \alpha I)^k v\text{,} \end{align*}
    where \(b_0 = 1\) and the values of the other binomial coefficients \(b_k\) do not matter. Apply the operator \((T - \alpha I)^{m-1}\) to both sides of the equation above, getting
    \begin{equation*} 0 = (\alpha - \lambda)^n (T - \alpha I)^{m-1} v. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Because \((T - \alpha I)^{m-1} v \ne 0\text{,}\) the equation above implies that \(\alpha = \lambda\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    🔗
    We saw earlier Theorem 5.11 that eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. Now we prove a similar result for generalized eigenvectors, with a proof that roughly follows the pattern of the proof of that earlier result.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.12. linearly independent generalized eigenvectors.

    Suppose that \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then every list of generalized eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of \(T\) is linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose the desired result is false. Then there exists a smallest positive integer \(m\) such that there exists a linearly dependent list \(v_1, \ldots, v_m\) of generalized eigenvectors of \(T\) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_m\) of \(T\) (note that \(m\ge 2\) because a generalized eigenvector is, by definition, nonzero). Thus there exist \(a_1, \ldots, a_m \in \F{}{}\text{,}\) none of which are \(0\) (because of the minimality of \(m\)), such that
    \begin{equation*} a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_m v_m = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let \(n = \dim V{}\text{.}\) Apply \((T - \lambda_m I)^n\) to both sides of the equation above, getting
    \begin{equation} a_1 (T - \lambda_m I)^n v_1 + \cdots + a_{m-1} (T - \lambda_m I)^n v_{m-1} = 0.\tag{8.13} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Suppose \(k \in \{ 1, \ldots, m-1 \}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} (T - \lambda_m I)^n v_k \ne 0 \end{equation*}
    because otherwise \(v_k\) would be a generalized eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to the distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\) and \(\lambda_m\text{,}\) which would contradict 8.11. However,
    \begin{equation*} (T - \lambda_k I)^n ( (T - \lambda_m I)^n v_k ) = (T - \lambda_m I)^n ( (T - \lambda_k I)^n v_k ) = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus the last two displayed equations show that \((T - \lambda_m I)^n v_k\) is a generalized eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(\lambda_k\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} (T - \lambda_m I)^n v_1, \ldots, (T - \lambda_m I)^n v_{m-1} \end{equation*}
    is a linearly dependent list (by (8.13)) of \(m-1\) generalized eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues, contradicting the minimality of \(m\text{.}\) This contradiction completes the proof.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.1.3 Nilpotent Operators

    Definition 8.14. nilpotent.

    An operator is called nilpotent if some power of it equals \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus an operator \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is nilpotent if and only if every nonzero vector in \(V\) is a generalized eigenvector of \(T\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 8.15. nilpotent operators.

    1. The operator \(T \in \Lc( \F{4} )\) defined by
      \begin{equation*} T(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) = (0, 0, z_1, z_2) \end{equation*}
      is nilpotent because \(T^2 = 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The operator on \(\F3\) whose matrix (with respect to the standard basis) is
      \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}-3 \amp 9 \amp 0 \\ -7 \amp 9 \amp 6 \\ 4 \amp 0 \amp -6 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation*}
      is nilpotent, as can be shown by cubing the matrix above to get the zero matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The operator of differentiation on \(\P{}_m(\R{})\) is nilpotent because the \((m + 1)^{\nth}\) derivative of every polynomial of degree at most \(m\) equals \(0\text{.}\) Note that on this space of dimension \(m + 1\text{,}\) we need to raise the nilpotent operator to the power \(m + 1\) to get the \(0\) operator.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The Latin word nil means nothing or zero; the Latin word potens means having power. Thus nilpotent literally means having a power that is zero.
    🔗
    The next result shows that when raising a nilpotent operator to a power, we never need to use a power higher than the dimension of the space. For a slightly stronger result, see Exercise 8.1.3.18.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.16. nilpotent operator raised to dimension of domain is \(0\).

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\) is nilpotent. Then \(T^{\dim V} = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Because \(T\) is nilpotent, there exists a positive integer \(k\) such that \(T^k = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(\ker T^k = V{}\text{.}\) Now 8.1 and Theorem 8.3 imply that \(\ker T^{\dim V} = V{}\text{.}\) Thus \(T^{\dim V} = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.17. eigenvalues of nilpotent operator.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\)
    1. If \(T\) is nilpotent, then \(0\) is an eigenvalue of \(T\) and \(T\) has no other eigenvalues.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(\F{} = \C{}\) and \(0\) is the only eigenvalue of \(T\text{,}\) then \(T\) is nilpotent.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. To prove (a), suppose \(T\) is nilpotent. Hence there is a positive integer \(m\) such that \(T^m = 0\text{.}\) This implies that \(T\) is not injective. Thus \(0\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) To show that \(T\) has no other eigenvalues, suppose \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) Then there exists a nonzero vector \(v \in V\) such that
      \begin{equation*} \lambda v = Tv. \end{equation*}
      Repeatedly applying \(T\) to both sides of this equation shows that
      \begin{equation*} \lambda^m v = T^m v = 0. \end{equation*}
      Thus \(\lambda = 0\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(\F{} = \C{}\) and \(0\) is the only eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) By 5.27(b), the minimal polynomial of \(T\) equals \(z^m\) for some positive integer \(m\text{.}\) Thus \(T^m = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(T\) is nilpotent.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Exercise 8.1.3.23 shows that the hypothesis that \(\F{} = \C{}\) cannot be deleted in (b) of the result above.
    🔗
    Given an operator on \(V{}\text{,}\) we want to find a basis of \(V\) such that the matrix of the operator with respect to this basis is as simple as possible, meaning that the matrix contains many \(0\)’s. The next result shows that if \(T\) is nilpotent, then we can choose a basis of \(V\) such that the matrix of \(T\) with respect to this basis has more than half of its entries equal to \(0\text{.}\) Later in this chapter we will do even better.
    🔗

    Theorem 8.18. minimal polynomial and upper-triangular matrix of nilpotent operator.

    Suppose \(T \in \Lc(V)\text{.}\) Then the following are equivalent.
    1. \(T\) is nilpotent.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The minimal polynomial of \(T\) is \(z^m\) for some positive integer \(m\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. There is a basis of \(V\) with respect to which the matrix of \(T\) has the form
      \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix}0 \amp \amp * \\ \amp \ddots \amp \\ 0 \amp \amp 0 \end{pmatrix} , \end{equation*}
      where all entries on and below the diagonal equal \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Suppose (a) holds, so \(T\) is nilpotent. Thus there exists a positive integer \(n\) such that \(T^n = 0\text{.}\) Now 5.29 implies that \(z^n\) is a polynomial multiple of the minimal polynomial of \(T{}\text{.}\) Thus the minimal polynomial of \(T\) is \(z^m\) for some positive integer \(m\text{,}\) proving that (a) implies (b).
    🔗
    Now suppose (b) holds, so the minimal polynomial of \(T\) is \(z^m\) for some positive integer \(m\text{.}\) This implies, by Theorem 5.27(a), that \(0\) \big(which is the only zero of \(z^m\)\big) is the only eigenvalue of \(T{}\text{.}\) This further implies, by Theorem 5.44, that there is a basis of \(V\) with respect to which the matrix of \(T\) is upper triangular. This also implies, by Theorem 5.41, that all entries on the diagonal of this matrix are \(0\text{,}\) proving that (b) implies (c).
    🔗
    Now suppose (c) holds. Then 5.40 implies that \(T^{\dim V} = 0\text{.}\) Thus \(T\) is nilpotent, proving that (c) implies (a).
    🔗
